FDCPA and Regulation F Rules for Rent Collection

FDCPA and Regulation F rules for rent collection aren't optional guidance a collector can interpret loosely — they're specific, enforceable requirements covering what a collector must disclose, how often it can make contact, and what a resident can do if those rules are broken.

Shawn Juarez, Managing Director, US Collections and Growth · Published August 3, 2026
Quick answer

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) and its implementing rule, Regulation F, govern how a third-party agency can pursue unpaid rent once it's placed for collection. A validation notice with an itemized breakdown of the balance must go out within 5 days of first contact, contact is capped at roughly 7 attempts per 7-day period, calls are generally limited to 8 a.m.–9 p.m. local time, and residents can sue for up to $1,000 in statutory damages per violation regardless of actual harm (FTC, FDCPA compliance guidance).

What is the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, and does it apply to rent?

Unpaid rent becomes federally regulated debt the moment it's placed with a third-party collector.

The FDCPA is the federal law governing how third-party debt collectors — as opposed to the original creditor collecting its own debt directly — can pursue consumer debt (Cornell Law, FDCPA overview). Once a rent balance is placed with a specialist agency like Elite Recovery Group rather than chased in-house, that placement puts the account squarely under FDCPA rules, along with Regulation F, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's 2021 rule that clarified and modernized how those rules apply in practice.

What did Regulation F change for debt collectors?

Regulation F turned decades of case-law interpretation into specific, numeric rules.

Regulation F set concrete limits where the original 1977 statute had left room for interpretation: a defined cap on call frequency, a formal itemization requirement for the validation notice, and — for the first time — explicit rules for email, text, and other digital contact (CFPB, Regulation F). For a rent collections placement specifically, this is the rulebook that governs every notice sent and every contact attempt made.

What must a validation notice for unpaid rent include?

The resident gets the full accounting in writing before collection activity really begins.

Within 5 days of initial contact, the collector must send a validation notice disclosing the amount owed, the current creditor's name, an itemized breakdown of the balance, an itemization date anchoring those figures, and a clear statement of the resident's right to dispute the debt (CFPB, Regulation F). Collectors using the CFPB's model notice format get a safe harbor on these disclosure requirements specifically — one more reason a compliance-first agency builds its process around the model notice rather than a custom one.

How often can a collector contact a resident about back rent?

There's a hard numeric ceiling, and it applies per debt, not per resident.

Regulation F presumes that more than 7 calls within a 7-day period about the same debt constitutes harassment, and requires at least a 7-day wait after a phone conversation before calling again about that debt (CFPB, Regulation F). That "7-in-7" limit is tracked per account, which matters for a portfolio where one resident might have more than one balance placed — a compliant agency has to track call attempts at the account level, not just the person.

What are the rules for contacting a resident by email or text?

Digital contact is allowed, but it isn't unregulated just because it isn't a phone call.

Regulation F extended FDCPA protections to email, text messages, and other digital channels, and requires that residents be able to opt out of a given channel through a simple, clearly disclosed method (CFPB, Regulation F). Content and frequency limits apply to digital contact the same way they apply to calls — switching channels isn't a way around the contact rules.

What can't a debt collector do when pursuing unpaid rent?

The restrictions cover timing, tone, and who else gets to hear about the debt.

Outside of the notice and frequency rules, the FDCPA prohibits contacting a resident before 8:00 a.m. or after 9:00 p.m. in their local time zone, contacting them at work if the employer prohibits it, discussing the debt with third parties beyond limited exceptions, and any false, deceptive, or harassing representation about the debt or the consequences of not paying it (FTC, FDCPA compliance guidance). A resident can also send a written request to stop contact entirely, which the collector must honor except for a narrow set of legally required notices.

What happens if a collection agency violates the FDCPA or Regulation F?

Non-compliance is a liability that belongs to whoever placed the account, not just the collector.

A resident can sue for statutory damages of up to $1,000 per lawsuit, regardless of whether they can show actual harm, plus any actual damages and attorney's fees, and regulators can bring separate enforcement action against the collector (FTC, FDCPA compliance guidance). For a property portfolio, that exposure is a direct argument for choosing a collection partner built around these rules from the start rather than one treating them as a compliance afterthought — see ERG's compliance and licensing standing for how that discipline is structured.

This is also why the notice and reporting timeline matters beyond just the collector's own liability — it shapes how and when an account can move toward credit-bureau reporting and dispute resolution once it's placed. Key takeaways
  • The FDCPA governs third-party collectors, and applies to a rent balance once it's placed with an agency (Cornell Law).
  • Regulation F requires a validation notice with an itemized balance within 5 days of first contact (CFPB).
  • Contact is capped at roughly 7 attempts per 7-day period per debt, tracked at the account level.
  • Email and text contact are permitted but require a simple, disclosed opt-out per channel.
  • Contact hours are generally limited to 8 a.m.–9 p.m. resident local time (FTC).
  • A written cease-communication request generally stops contact, with narrow legal exceptions.
  • Violations expose the collector — and by extension the placing portfolio — to statutory damages up to $1,000 per lawsuit, plus actual damages and attorney's fees.

Frequently asked questions

  • Does the FDCPA apply to unpaid rent?

    Yes, once a rent balance is placed with a third-party collection agency. The FDCPA governs debt collectors pursuing consumer debt on behalf of someone else, and unpaid rent qualifies as consumer debt once it's placed for collection.

  • What must a validation notice for unpaid rent include?

    The amount owed, the current creditor's name, an itemized breakdown of the balance, an itemization date, and a clear statement of the resident's right to dispute the debt, sent within 5 days of initial contact.

  • How many times can a collector call about back rent under Regulation F?

    Regulation F presumes that calling more than 7 times within a 7-day period about the same debt is harassment, and requires at least a 7-day wait after a phone conversation before calling again about that debt.

  • Can a debt collector text or email a resident about rent?

    Yes, Regulation F permits email and text contact, but the resident must be able to opt out of that channel with a simple, clearly disclosed method, and specific content and frequency rules apply.

  • What times of day can a collector contact a resident?

    Generally only between 8:00 a.m. and 9:00 p.m. in the resident's local time zone, unless the resident has agreed to a different window.

  • What happens if a collection agency violates the FDCPA or Regulation F?

    A resident can sue for statutory damages of up to $1,000 per lawsuit regardless of actual harm, plus actual damages and attorney's fees, and regulators can pursue separate enforcement action against the collector.
