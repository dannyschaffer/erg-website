How to Choose a Collection Agency for Property Management

Placing a resident's debt with the wrong agency doesn't just cost you a recovery; it exposes your management company to compliance and reputational risk you can't easily undo. Here is how to vet an agency on the things that actually matter before you sign.

Shawn Juarez, Managing Director, US Collections and Growth · Published August 24, 2026
Quick answer

Vet a property-management collection agency on six things beyond basic FDCPA compliance: industry certification (RMAI RMCP, ACA International), active licensing in every state your residents live, data security (SOC 2 Type II, PCI DSS if it takes card payments), a transparent contingency-fee schedule, genuine rental-debt specialization, and a clean complaint history in the CFPB Consumer Complaint Database. The checklist at the end turns this into a pre-signing process.

Certifications That Signal a Compliant Agency

Two credentials separate agencies that take compliance seriously from those that merely claim to: RMAI certification and ACA International membership.

The Receivables Management Association International runs the Receivables Management Certification Program (RMCP), often described as the receivables industry's gold standard because its standards go beyond what the law requires and, since March 2024, mandate an independent pre-certification audit. Its February 2026 Version 14.0 added requirements around cyber-insurance coverage, new-employee training, ADA website compliance, a code of ethics, and written AI-use policies. A certified business must also maintain a Chief Compliance Officer holding RMAI's Certified Receivables Compliance Professional (CRCP) credential. Separately, ACA International, the main U.S. trade association for collection professionals, binds its members to a code of ethics that, among other things, forbids suing on time-barred debt and requires accurate account information (ACA Code of Conduct). Ask any shortlisted agency whether it is RMAI-certified and an ACA member, and request the certificate or roster listing.

Verify Licensing State by State

Collection-agency licensing follows the resident's state, not the agency's, so a multi-state portfolio needs an agency authorized everywhere your residents live.

There is no national collection-agency license; it's governed state by state, and the obligation tracks where the consumer lives. An agency headquartered in one state generally still needs authority in each state where it contacts residents. Verify each license yourself rather than taking it on faith: check the resident state's financial regulator or licensing board, or use NMLS Consumer Access for states that license through NMLS, confirm the license is active, and check that the licensed entity name exactly matches the entity on your contract, a mismatch being one of the most common problems in vendor reviews. Note that the landscape shifts: some states never licensed collection agencies, and Utah repealed its registration and bond requirement entirely effective May 3, 2023, so aggregated licensing sites are sometimes out of date. For a portfolio spread across states, an agency already licensed across your footprint reduces the risk of an unlicensed-collection challenge. See Arizona Debt Collection Laws and Statute of Limitations on Rent Debt for how much the rules vary by state.

Data Security You Should Demand Proof Of

You're handing an agency your residents' personal and financial data, so treat security like the material risk it is.

Two standards are worth asking about by name. A SOC 2 report evaluates an agency's controls against the AICPA's Trust Services Criteria; a Type II report is the one that matters, because it attests that controls operated effectively over a period of months, not just that they were designed on a single date. Ask for a recent Type II covering at least Security, and ideally Confidentiality and Privacy given the tenant data involved. If the agency takes card payments, PCI DSS 4.0.1 (the current version, fully mandatory since March 2025) governs how cardholder data is stored and transmitted, and the agency should be able to show an Attestation of Compliance. Beyond the paperwork, ask concrete questions about encryption at rest and in transit, multi-factor authentication, access controls, and incident response.

Understand the Fee Model Before You Sign

Most agencies work on contingency, but the headline percentage is only part of the economics.

Contingency, or "no recovery, no fee," is the standard model, and account age is the dominant driver of the rate: fresher accounts cost less, older ones more. General agencies often charge 25 to 50 percent, while rental-debt specialists sometimes run lower on newer accounts. The rate alone doesn't tell you the real cost, though. Scrutinize add-on fees (setup, skip-tracing, credit-reporting, or "legal management" charges), find out who bears litigation and court costs, and confirm there's no minimum or monthly charge that quietly guarantees the agency revenue on accounts it never collects. Get the whole schedule in writing. For a full breakdown of how these fees are structured, see How Much Do Collection Agencies Charge?.

Reporting, Integration, and Real Specialization

The best agency for a property manager reports in a format you can hand to owners, and knows rental debt specifically.

You'll manage this relationship through its reporting, so require a portfolio-level view showing placements, recoveries, statuses, and remittances, with exports clean enough to fold into owner reporting. Favor agencies that integrate with your property-management software, or at minimum support secure API or SFTP data exchange that maps owner, property, unit, and resident identifiers correctly. Specialization is the quieter differentiator: an agency fluent in landlord-tenant law, eviction timelines, and security-deposit rules works rental and move-out balances more effectively and with fewer compliance missteps than a generalist. Ask for recovery statistics on portfolios like yours, segmented by age at placement and property type, rather than generic consumer-debt averages. For the underlying case, see In-House Collections vs. Outsourcing to a Specialist Agency.

Red Flags and Checking Complaint History

Before you sign, spend ten minutes on the agency's public record. The CFPB Consumer Complaint Database is searchable by company under the "Debt collection" product; a pattern of complaints alleging "attempts to collect debt not owed," harassment, or threats of time-barred lawsuits signals systemic data or conduct problems. Cross-check the agency's Better Business Bureau profile and look for state attorney general enforcement actions. The clearest red flags: unwillingness to provide licensing or bonding evidence, expired or mismatched licenses, no certifications or code of ethics, no named compliance officer, and a vague or absent data-security posture. Any one of these is a reason to keep looking.

Pre-signing vetting checklist
  • Confirm RMAI RMCP certification and ACA International membership, plus a named CRCP compliance officer.
  • Verify active licensing in every state your residents live, matching the exact contracting entity.
  • Request a recent SOC 2 Type II report, and PCI DSS 4.0.1 attestation if the agency takes card payments.
  • Get the full contingency-fee schedule in writing, including add-ons and who bears litigation costs.
  • Require portfolio-level reporting and confirm software integration or secure data exchange.
  • Ask for rental-specific recovery stats by account age and property type.
  • Search the CFPB database, BBB, and state AG actions for the agency's complaint and enforcement history.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • What certifications should a property management collection agency have?

    Look for RMAI Receivables Management Certification (RMCP), often called the industry's gold standard because it requires an independent audit and standards beyond the law, and membership in ACA International, which binds an agency to a code of ethics. A strong agency also has a named Chief Compliance Officer with RMAI's CRCP credential.

  • How do I verify a collection agency is licensed in my state?

    Licensing generally follows the resident's state, not the agency's, so check the regulator in each state where your residents live. Use NMLS Consumer Access for states that license through NMLS, or the state's financial regulator or licensing board otherwise, and confirm the license is active and matches the exact entity you're contracting with. Note that some states, such as Utah since 2023, have repealed collection-agency licensing entirely.

  • What data-security standards should a collection agency meet?

    Ask for a recent SOC 2 Type II report, which shows the agency's data controls operated effectively over a period of months rather than at a single date, covering at least Security and ideally Confidentiality and Privacy. If the agency accepts card payments, ask for PCI DSS 4.0.1 attestation. You're handing over resident names, addresses, and financial data, so these are reasonable things to demand proof of.

  • What is a typical contingency fee for property management collections?

    Most agencies work on contingency (no recovery, no fee), with rates driven mainly by account age. General agencies often charge 25 to 50 percent, while agencies that specialize in rental debt sometimes run lower, around 10 to 25 percent on fresher accounts. Scrutinize add-on fees and who bears litigation costs, and get the full schedule in writing.

  • Why choose a rental-debt specialist over a general collection agency?

    A specialist understands landlord-tenant law, eviction procedures, security-deposit rules, and lease structures, which lets it work rental and move-out balances more effectively and with fewer compliance missteps. Property-management portfolios also tend to recover somewhat better than the general average because leases are documented and enforceable, an advantage a specialist is best positioned to use.

  • How can I check a collection agency's complaint history?

    Search the CFPB Consumer Complaint Database by company name under the Debt collection product, and watch for patterns like "attempts to collect debt not owed." Also check the agency's Better Business Bureau profile and look for state attorney general enforcement actions. A pattern of complaints alleging debt-not-owed, harassment, or threats of time-barred lawsuits is a serious red flag.
