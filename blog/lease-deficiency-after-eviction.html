Lease Deficiency After Eviction: Collecting What the Judgment Didn't Cover

An eviction judgment gets you the unit back and sometimes the rent owed up to the hearing. It rarely covers everything the lease says the tenant owes: rent until the unit re-lets, re-letting costs, lease-break fees and damage beyond the deposit. That remainder is the lease deficiency. This guide covers what you can still collect in Texas, Arizona, Utah and Oregon, and how to document it so it holds up. It's general information, not legal advice.

Shawn Juarez, Managing Director, US Collections and Growth · LinkedIn · Published October 29, 2026 · 12 min read
Quick answer

You can usually still collect a lease deficiency after an eviction, but only the part the judgment didn't decide and only what mitigation leaves. A Texas eviction judgment covers rent through the judgment date if the claim is $20,000 or less (Tex. R. Civ. P. 510); Utah's statute says lease forfeiture doesn't release the tenant from the rest of the term. All four states require reasonable efforts to re-rent, so future rent is the vacancy months plus any shortfall, not the whole remaining lease. Plead what's due in the eviction, document re-letting, meet the deposit deadline, and pursue the rest by civil suit or a collection agency.

What Counts as a Lease Deficiency

A lease deficiency is everything the former tenant still owes under the lease after the eviction is over. It usually includes:

  • Unpaid rent before judgment that the eviction judgment didn't include.
  • Rent or use-and-occupancy after judgment, until you got possession back.
  • Future rent under the lease, reduced by what the replacement tenant pays and limited by your duty to mitigate.
  • Reasonable re-letting costs: advertising, listing fees and turnover directly tied to finding a new tenant.
  • Early termination or lease-break fees, if the lease clearly allows them and they're a fair estimate of loss rather than a penalty.
  • Damage beyond the security deposit, backed by invoices, photos and an on-time deposit accounting.
  • Interest and attorney fees, only where the lease or a statute allows them.

The difference between a collectible deficiency and a dispute is documentation. A judge, a collector or a tenant's attorney will look first at the lease, the ledger, the deposit accounting and the proof that you tried to re-rent. Before any of that, though, you need to know what the eviction court already decided.

What the Eviction Judgment Covers in Each State

An eviction judgment is mostly about possession. Each state lets the court add some money, but none of them automatically covers the rest of the lease term.

Utah stands out. Under 78B-6-811, treble damages are mandatory once unlawful detainer is established; the Utah Supreme Court said the statute leaves no room for an exception (Martin v. Kristensen). Utah Legal Services explains that trebling applies to the unlawful detainer period and some other categories, not to every dollar owed (Utah Legal Services). The same statute says forfeiting the lease doesn't release the tenant from payments for the rest of the term, and it keeps both sides' duty to mitigate. That makes a later suit for the remaining rent possible, as long as you avoid one trap.

The Claim-Splitting Trap

If you could have claimed an amount in the eviction case and didn't, the tenant may argue you can't sue for it later. That defense is called res judicata, or claim splitting. How strong it is depends on the state, on what you pleaded and on what the judgment says, so treat it as a legal strategy decision, not paperwork.

  • Texas: Rule 510 lets you join a rent claim of $20,000 or less in the eviction. It doesn't say leaving rent out bars a later suit, but a tenant can still argue it. If the rent fits under the limit, plead it, or get the rent claim dismissed in writing before judgment. If it's over $20,000, record that the justice court couldn't hear the whole claim and file the balance separately.
  • Arizona: plead every amount due and calculable at the time of the eviction. Save future rent and damages you find later for a separate breach-of-lease case, with a ledger that splits the periods before judgment, after judgment and after re-letting.
  • Utah: the risk is highest, because the eviction court can award "amounts due under the contract." Keep possession, rent through surrender, holdover damages, waste and amounts then due in the eviction case, and only later-arising amounts for a separate one.
  • Oregon: an FED case is mostly about possession, so a separate civil action for rent and damages is the normal route. Just don't relitigate what the FED court actually decided.

Whatever the state, the biggest limit on a deficiency claim isn't procedure. It's mitigation.

Your Duty to Mitigate Caps Future Rent

All four states make the landlord try to re-rent, and a landlord who doesn't can't charge the old tenant for the empty months.

In Arizona and Oregon, if you re-let before the old lease ends, the old lease ends on the new tenancy's start date. If you don't make reasonable efforts, the lease is treated as ending when you knew or should have known about the abandonment (A.R.S. 33-1370; ORS 90.410). Texas imposes the duty in Property Code 91.006 and voids any lease clause that waives it (Tex. Prop. Code 91.006). Utah's 78B-6-811 keeps the duty in place after an eviction.

So the collectible future rent is the old rent minus the replacement rent, for a reasonable vacancy period, plus documented re-letting costs. An acceleration clause that claims the whole remaining term is unlikely to hold up when you could have re-rented, and in Texas it also has to survive penalty and liquidated damages rules. Building a Deficiency File That Holds Up

Assume a tenant's attorney, a judge or a credit bureau will audit the file. Two records do most of the work.

The final ledger

  • Lease dates, monthly rent and recurring fees.
  • Every charge, payment, credit, concession and waiver.
  • Rent through the judgment date, then daily rent or use-and-occupancy after it.
  • The replacement tenant's rent and start date, and the net future-rent loss.
  • Interest, only if the lease allows it.

The ledger should tie exactly to the judgment, the deposit accounting and any demand letter.

Proof you tried to re-rent

  • Listing dates and where the unit was advertised.
  • Advertising invoices and showing records.
  • Applicant communications, and the reason for any denial.
  • The replacement lease, move-in date and rent.
  • Turnover invoices tied to marketing or preparing the unit.

In Arizona and Oregon, the statutes make re-letting evidence part of the claim itself. The deposit accounting matters just as much: a late or defective one invites a counterclaim and undercuts the damage portion. Deadlines run from move-out, not from the judgment: 30 days in Texas and Utah, 14 business days in Arizona and 31 days in Oregon. Picking the Itemization Date for a Deficiency

If you place the deficiency with a collection agency, the agency's validation notice must itemize the debt from one of five reference dates: the last statement date, charge-off date, last payment date, transaction date or judgment date (CFPB, 12 CFR 1006.34). It then shows interest, fees, payments and credits since that date (12 CFR Part 1006).

  • If a judgment fixed a money amount, the judgment date is usually the cleanest choice for that part.
  • For a deficiency not yet reduced to judgment, the last statement date or last payment date usually fits better.

Give the agency a final statement showing the balance on the chosen date and a line-by-line bridge from that date to today. Our explainer on the Reg F itemization date walks through each option. With the file built, you can pick how to collect.

Deadlines for Suing on a Deficiency

Use the deadline for the underlying lease claim, not the eviction date. Written lease claims generally have four years in Texas and six in Arizona and Utah. In Oregon, ORS 12.125 sets one year for actions arising under a rental agreement or ORS chapter 90 (ORS 12.125), so treat one year as the safe limit. Each piece can start its own clock: unpaid rent accrues monthly, future rent as each installment comes due, and damages at surrender or deposit accounting. Partial payments and written acknowledgments can change the math too. Our guide to the statute of limitations for rent debt has the details.

How Big Are Post-Eviction Balances?

Nobody publishes a reliable average. Eviction Lab tracks filings and judgments across the country, but court records measure cases, not the debt left after them (Eviction Lab). A deficiency can exist even when the eviction judgment was only for possession or rent through the judgment date, and the collectible amount is usually well below the remaining contract rent, because mitigation, replacement rent and deposit credits shrink it. Key takeaways
  • A lease deficiency is what the tenant still owes after the eviction: later rent, re-letting costs, lease-break fees, damage beyond the deposit, and authorized interest and fees.
  • Eviction judgments mostly decide possession; a Texas judgment covers rent only through the judgment date, and only up to $20,000.
  • Plead every amount then due in the eviction, or you risk a claim-splitting defense when you sue later; Utah's risk is highest.
  • Mitigation caps future rent in all four states; in Arizona and Oregon, re-letting ends the old lease on the new tenancy's start date.
  • Acceleration clauses claiming the whole remaining term rarely survive when you could have re-rented.
  • Build the file around a reconciled final ledger, re-letting proof and an on-time deposit accounting.
  • Calendar the deadline: 4 years in Texas, 6 in Arizona and Utah, and treat 1 year as safe in Oregon.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Can I collect rent for the rest of the lease after evicting a tenant?

    Usually some of it, not all of it. You can claim rent for the time the unit sat empty while you made reasonable efforts to re-rent, plus documented re-letting costs. Claiming the full remaining term rarely holds up.

  • Are lease-break fees collectible after an eviction?

    Only if the lease clearly authorizes them and they're a reasonable estimate of your loss rather than a penalty. Combining a lease-break fee with full future rent can look like double recovery.

  • Does the security deposit have to be applied before claiming a deficiency?

    Yes. Account for the deposit within your state's deadline and apply it to what's owed. The deficiency is what remains after the deposit, and a late accounting can invite a counterclaim.

  • Can I charge an evicted tenant for advertising and turnover?

    You can claim reasonable re-letting costs directly tied to finding a replacement tenant, such as advertising and listing fees, if you keep invoices. Ordinary make-ready and normal wear and tear usually aren't recoverable.

  • Do Utah's treble damages apply to the whole deficiency?

    No. Utah Code 78B-6-811 trebles the damages assessed in the unlawful detainer case, such as rent during the unlawful detainer period and proven waste. Remaining-term rent pursued later is a separate contract claim.

  • Can a collection agency collect a deficiency that isn't a court judgment?

    Yes. A documented lease balance is a contract debt, and an agency can collect it with the lease, ledger and deposit accounting. The validation notice will itemize it from a statement or payment date rather than a judgment date.

  • Will a lease deficiency show up on the former tenant's credit report?

    Only if a collection agency or other furnisher reports it as a collection account. Civil judgments haven't appeared on consumer credit reports since 2017, so a judgment alone won't show.

  • Can I charge interest on a lease deficiency?

    Only if the lease or a statute allows it, and only at a lawful rate. Unauthorized interest is one of the fastest ways to turn a valid balance into a dispute.
