Small Claims or a Collection Agency? How to Pursue a Former Tenant's Unpaid Rent

When a former tenant leaves owing rent, you have two realistic options: sue them in small claims yourself or hand the account to a collection agency. This guide compares suing a former tenant for unpaid rent with using a collection agency in Texas, Arizona, Utah and Oregon, including limits, fees, finding the tenant, what a judgment can and can't reach, and deadlines. It's general information, not legal advice.

Shawn Juarez, Managing Director, US Collections and Growth · LinkedIn · Published October 23, 2026 · 16 min read
Quick answer

Use small claims when you can serve the former tenant and know of an asset you could collect from after winning; use a collection agency when you can't find them, can't name an asset or have many accounts. Court costs about $50 to $250 up front per case and keeps the full balance if you collect. An agency costs nothing up front and typically keeps 20% to 40% of what it recovers, according to the National Apartment Association. Watch the limits: $5,000 in Arizona small claims, $10,000 in Oregon and $20,000 in Texas and Utah. Texas bars wage garnishment for rent, and Oregon may give you only one year to sue.

Small Claims or a Collection Agency: The Short Version

Sue in small claims when you know where the former tenant lives, you can prove the balance, and you know of a bank account, property or (outside Texas) a paycheck you could reach after you win. Place the account with a collection agency when you can't find the tenant, can't name an asset, or have more accounts than time. The court route keeps more of every dollar but makes you do the work and carry the risk that a judgment never gets paid. The agency route costs nothing up front and does the locating, but keeps a share of what it recovers.

That rule holds in all four states we cover, but the details shift a lot from one to the next. Texas won't let you garnish wages for rent debt. Arizona's small-claims cap is only $5,000. Oregon may give you just one year to file. The rest of this guide walks through those differences so you can make the call account by account.

Small Claims Limits, Fees and Who Can Appear in TX, AZ, UT and OR

Small claims is cheap to file in every state, but the dollar caps and the rules on who can stand up in court differ enough to change your choice. The biggest surprise for most property managers: an outside management company usually can't represent the owner in a separate small-claims case, even though it handled the eviction.

Two details in that table cause most of the trouble. First, Texas and Utah count claimed attorney fees toward the cap, so a lease fee clause can push a large balance over the limit. Second, where you file is not always where the unit is. Texas lets you sue where the tenant lives or where the lease was performed, and if the tenant's home is unknown or out of state, Rule 502.4 lets you file in your own county (Texas State Law Library). Oregon starts from the tenant's county but allows the county where the tenant agreed to perform, which usually means the rental. Our state law guides for Texas, Arizona, Utah and Oregon cover the rest of each state's rules. Filing is the easy part, though. Getting the papers to someone who has moved is where most court cases stall.

Serving a Former Tenant Who Has Moved Out

A small-claims case can't move until the former tenant is served, and you need a current address to serve them. Every state allows certified mail or a constable or process server. All four also allow alternative service, such as email, text or publication, but only after you show the court you made a real effort and failed.

  • Texas: citation goes by sheriff, constable, process server or certified mail under Rule 501.2. Courts expect documented attempts first; one Tarrant County justice court wants tries on at least two different days and times before it will consider alternative service.
  • Arizona: certified mail with a return receipt, or a constable, sheriff or process server. Proof of service must be filed within 45 days of filing or the case can be dismissed (AZCourtHelp).
  • Utah: sheriff, constable, professional server, or signature-required mail or courier, at least 30 days before trial. If the tenant can't be found, the court can order email, text or social-media notice.
  • Oregon: personal service, substituted service at the home, office service or qualifying mail under ORCP 7, with alternative service by court order.

If you don't have an address, you'll pay for a locate first. No government source publishes a typical price; current process-server rate cards run from about $39 for a basic address search to $149 to $199 for a fuller report, so budget $50 to $200 and get a written quote (Served123 rate card, 2026). A database hit is a lead, not proof the tenant lives there. This is exactly the step a collection agency does as standard: locating a moved-out resident is most of what collecting after move-out involves. And even a perfectly served case only gets you a judgment, which is not the same thing as money.

Winning a Judgment Is Not the Same as Getting Paid

A judgment proves the tenant owes you. It doesn't find their bank, their employer or their property, and the court won't collect for you. No court, government or academic dataset tracks how many small-claims or landlord judgments are ever paid, so be wary of anyone quoting a national percentage.

What you can do after winning depends heavily on the state:

Texas is the outlier. A steady job doesn't help a Texas landlord collect a rent judgment, so you need to know about a bank account, nonexempt property or non-homestead real estate before you sue. In the other three states a known employer makes the court route far more attractive. Our guide to eviction judgment collection covers enforcement in more depth. Credit reporting is the other place people assume a judgment will do the work for them.

A Court Judgment No Longer Shows Up on a Credit Report

The three national credit bureaus stopped putting civil judgments on consumer credit files in July 2017 under the National Consumer Assistance Plan (FDIC-hosted research, 2020). So winning in small claims puts nothing on the former tenant's credit report, and it gives them no credit reason to pay.

The same balance can still appear as a collection account if a collection agency reports it under the FCRA and Regulation F. Before reporting, the agency has to contact the consumer, or send a message and wait a reasonable period, generally 14 days, to see if it bounces (CFPB compliance guide). In the CFPB's rental payment dataset, about 7,700 renters had collections placed in the 12 months to November 2024, totaling roughly $37 million, with a median of about $2,600 (CFPB, 2025). That median sits squarely in small-claims range, which is why the cost comparison below uses balances of $2,500 and $7,500.

How Much Does Each Route Cost?

Court costs you $50 to $250 up front per case before you've collected anything, plus your time. An agency costs nothing up front and takes a share of what it collects. The National Apartment Association says multifamily collection agencies generally charge nothing up front and keep about 20% to 40% of what they recover (NAA), in line with the 19.5% to 35% contracted rates a federal audit found for private debt counsel (GAO, 1994). No neutral study isolates a current rate for rental debt, so treat those as planning ranges.

On paper the court wins easily: $134 to keep the whole $2,500 instead of giving up $500 to $1,000. But the court figure assumes three things: you serve the tenant, you win, and then you actually collect. The honest comparison is what you expect to keep. For court, that's your realistic chance of being paid after judgment, times the balance, minus filing, service, locate and enforcement costs. For an agency, it's the agency's recovery rate on comparable rental accounts, times the balance, times what's left after its fee. What a Collection Agency Needs and Must Do

An agency can only collect what your file proves, so the placement package decides how much comes back. The National Apartment Association recommends sending the final account statement showing how the deposit was applied, the signed lease and addenda, any guaranties, move-in and move-out inspections, repair invoices and current contact details (NAA multifamily collections guide). Our placement checklist lists each one, and a late deposit itemization is the easiest way to lose a dispute, as our security deposit guide explains.

Placing also changes which laws apply. A landlord collecting its own debt in its own name generally isn't a "debt collector" under the FDCPA. A third-party agency is, so it has to follow the FDCPA and Regulation F (CFPB):

  • Send a validation notice with the creditor, itemized amount and 30-day dispute window, in the first communication or within five days (12 CFR 1006.34).
  • Stop collecting a timely disputed amount until it sends verification or a copy of a judgment.
  • Collect only amounts the lease or the law authorizes. The CFPB received more than 1,700 rental debt complaints between August and December 2023 and has flagged fees added on top of rent (CFPB, 2024).

The landlord exemption isn't a free pass. State collection laws, landlord-tenant rules and credit-reporting law still apply when you collect yourself, as our FDCPA and Regulation F guide explains. For many portfolios, though, the best answer isn't one route or the other.

When to Use Both, and in Which Order

You can place an account with an agency first and sue later, or win a judgment first and then place it. The order should follow your biggest unknown: if you can't find the tenant, start with the agency; if you can find them and they have assets, start with the court.

  • Agency first works when locating the tenant and finding an employer or bank is the main problem. Check the placement contract for whether you can recall the account, whether the agency can authorize a lawsuit, whether the fee changes after suit, and whether payments made directly to you still earn the agency a commission.
  • Court first works when service is straightforward, assets are known and the file proves the balance without much testimony. After judgment, you can hand the judgment to an agency to collect, which is common for eviction judgments.
  • Attorney-fee clauses help the court route if the case is contested, but in Texas and Utah they count toward the $20,000 cap.

Whichever order you choose, calendar the filing deadline yourself. Letters, calls and payment talks don't stop the clock, and in one of our four states the clock is much shorter than most managers think.

Filing Deadlines: Oregon Is the One to Watch

Texas gives you four years to sue on a debt, and Arizona and Utah give six years on a written lease. Oregon has a one-year statute for actions under a rental agreement, so treat one year from the charge as your deadline there unless an Oregon lawyer tells you the six-year contract period applies.

Key takeaways
  • Small claims fits when you can serve the former tenant and know of a bank account, property or (outside Texas) an employer to collect from after you win.
  • A collection agency fits when you can't find the tenant, can't name an asset, or have more accounts than staff time.
  • Small-claims limits are $5,000 in Arizona, $10,000 in Oregon and $20,000 in Texas and Utah; Texas and Utah count attorney fees toward the cap.
  • An outside property management company usually can't represent the owner in a separate small-claims case in any of the four states.
  • A judgment won't appear on a credit report (bureaus dropped them in 2017), and in Texas it can't reach wages.
  • Agencies typically keep 20% to 40% of what they recover and charge nothing up front; compare expected net, not the fee alone.
  • Calendar the deadline yourself: 4 years in Texas, 6 in Arizona and Utah on written leases, and treat 1 year as the safe limit in Oregon.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Can I sue a former tenant for unpaid rent in small claims?

    Yes, as long as the balance is under your state's limit: $5,000 in Arizona, $10,000 in Oregon and $20,000 in Texas and Utah. You file in the right county or precinct, serve the former tenant, and attend the hearing with the lease, ledger and move-out statement.

  • How much does it cost to sue a former tenant in small claims?

    Usually about $50 to $250 per case up front for filing and service. Examples: $54 to file plus $80 constable service in Fort Bend County, Texas; $57 or $102 to file in Oregon depending on the amount; $60, $100 or $185 to file in Utah. Locating a tenant who has moved typically adds $50 to $200.

  • Can a landlord hire a collection agency for unpaid rent?

    Yes. Landlords and property managers commonly place former-resident balances with a third-party agency on contingency. The agency must follow the FDCPA and Regulation F, including sending a validation notice, and can collect only amounts the lease or the law allows.

  • Can a property manager represent the owner in small claims?

    Usually not in a separate money case. Texas lets an outside property manager appear only in an eviction; for a small-claims case the company needs an employee, owner, officer or partner. Arizona, Utah and Oregon similarly require an officer, authorized employee or proper legal representative of the owner.

  • What if I don't know where my former tenant lives?

    You'll need to locate them before a court case can move, typically through a skip trace costing about $50 to $200, and courts in all four states allow alternative service only after documented failed attempts. This is the situation where placing the account with a collection agency usually makes more sense.

  • Can I garnish a former tenant's wages for unpaid rent in Texas?

    No. The Texas Constitution bars garnishing current wages for ordinary consumer debt, including rent. A Texas landlord with a judgment generally has to collect from a bank account, nonexempt property or a lien on non-homestead real estate.

  • Does a small claims judgment show up on a credit report?

    No. The national credit bureaus stopped reporting civil judgments in July 2017. A rental balance can still appear as a collection account if a collection agency furnishes it in line with the FCRA and Regulation F.

  • Can I send an account to collections after winning a judgment?

    Yes. Many landlords win the judgment and then place it with an agency to enforce. Check your placement contract for the fee on judgment accounts and whether the agency can pursue garnishment or liens in your state.

  • How long do I have to sue a former tenant for unpaid rent?

    Four years in Texas, and six years in Arizona and Utah on a written lease. In Oregon, ORS 12.125 sets one year for actions arising under a rental agreement, so file within a year unless an Oregon lawyer confirms the six-year contract period applies to your claim.
