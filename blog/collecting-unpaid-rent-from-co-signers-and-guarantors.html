Collecting Unpaid Rent from a Co-Signer or Lease Guarantor

A co-signer or guarantor looks like a second chance at the money. Whether it is depends on what that person actually signed, which state you're in, and whether you followed the right steps before and after the eviction. This guide covers who you can collect from first, how each state treats lease guaranties, and how collection rules protect guarantors and parents. It's general information, not legal advice.

Shawn Juarez, Managing Director, US Collections and Growth · LinkedIn · Published November 6, 2026 · 10 min read
Quick answer

It depends on what they signed. A co-signer on the lease is usually jointly and severally liable, so you can collect the whole balance from them. A guarantor who signed a guaranty of payment can be pursued as soon as the tenant defaults; a guaranty of collection makes you exhaust remedies against the tenant first (BBVA USA v. Francis). An eviction judgment against the tenant generally doesn't bind a separate guarantor, so plan a separate claim. A parent who signed nothing owes nothing and is a protected third party under Regulation F.

Co-Signer, Co-Tenant or Guarantor: Why the Label Matters

People use "co-signer" and "guarantor" interchangeably. The law doesn't, and the difference decides who you can collect from and when.

"Joint and several" means you can collect the whole obligation from any one liable party, who can then seek contribution from the others (LexisNexis). A guarantor's liability depends on one more distinction: whether they guaranteed payment or collection.

  • Guaranty of payment: unconditional. Once the tenant defaults, you can go straight to the guarantor without suing the tenant first. A Texas appeals court described a payment guaranty this way, with the tenant's default as the only condition (BBVA USA v. Francis).
  • Guaranty of collection: conditional. The guarantor owes nothing until you've used due diligence and failed to collect from the tenant, which usually means a judgment and attempted execution first (Minnesota State Bar, Bench & Bar).

Courts read the operative language, not the title. A document called "conditional" can still be a payment guaranty if its terms let you collect from the guarantor first (Lowenstein Sandler). If you want direct recourse, the guaranty should say it is "a guaranty of payment and not of collection" and that no demand, suit or judgment against the tenant is required.

State Rules in Texas, Arizona, Utah and Oregon

All four states require a promise to pay someone else's debt to be in writing and signed. A lease clause saying a parent "will be responsible" is weak support. A separately signed guaranty is better.

The Texas and Arizona "demand to sue" rules matter in practice. If a guarantor sends that written demand and you sit on the account, you can lose the guarantor entirely. Calendar the limitations date from each missed payment, not from the lease start. In Oregon, claims against the tenant under the landlord-tenant act can carry a much shorter limit than the guaranty itself; see our guides to the statute of limitations on rent debt and lease deficiency after eviction.

Does the Eviction Judgment Bind the Guarantor?

Generally not. An eviction judgment against the tenant is not a money judgment against a separate guarantor in any of the four states.

  • Arizona: guarantors can't be joined in the eviction case at all. In Fen v. Fonzi, the Court of Appeals vacated a judgment against guarantors in a special detainer action, while noting they could still be held liable in a separate civil action on the guaranty (Fen v. Fonzi).
  • Oregon: an eviction (FED) case is for possession only; back rent and damages need a separate small claims or civil case (Oregon Judicial Department).
  • Texas: a justice court eviction can include unpaid rent within the court's limit under Rule 510.3, but Harris County's justice courts say third-party joinder isn't allowed in eviction cases (Harris County JP). Plan on a separate debt claim against the guarantor.
  • Utah: unlawful detainer cases can include damages (URCP 26.3), so a properly joined and served guarantor might be bound. Don't assume it. If the guarantor wasn't named and the guaranty claim wasn't decided, sue on the guaranty separately.

A co-signer who signed the lease as a tenant is different. They're a lease party, can usually be named in the eviction, and are liable on the lease itself. That's one more reason to know which document each person signed. Our guide to small claims vs. a collection agency covers the separate money case.

How the FDCPA Treats Guarantors and Parents

Once a balance goes to a third-party collector, the guarantor is protected in their own right. The FDCPA defines a consumer as anyone obligated or allegedly obligated to pay a debt, which includes a guarantor or co-signer (CFPB debt collection FAQs). In practice:

  • Each obligor gets their own validation notice when the collector seeks payment from them, and their own 30-day dispute window.
  • A guarantor isn't a contact for the tenant. The collector can discuss the guarantor's own liability, not use them as a channel to the tenant.
  • Parents of adult students are third parties. Regulation F counts a parent as the consumer only if the consumer is a minor (12 CFR 1006.6). Unless the parent signed a guaranty, a collector can contact them only for location information, with the student's direct consent, or with court permission.
  • Each person's instructions stand alone. A cease request or attorney representation from one obligor doesn't bind the other.

Credit reporting follows the same logic. In the Metro 2 format, the primary tenant is usually reported with ECOA code 7 and a contingent guarantor or co-signer with code 5; code 2 is for true joint liability, such as two co-tenants who both signed as primary obligors (Metro 2 ECOA codes). Mislabeling a guarantor is a reporting dispute waiting to happen. Our Reg F validation notice guide covers what each notice must show.

Student Housing: Parent Guarantors and International Students

In by-the-bed student housing, a parent guarantor typically backs one resident's individual bed lease, not the roommates' beds. That keeps the guaranty narrow and makes it important to bill each bed's charges, especially shared damage, to the right resident. In the East Valley, for example, ASU-area landlords commonly require a co-signer for students under 23 (see our Mesa, Chandler, Gilbert and Tempe guide), and the same pattern runs through Austin and Provo.

International students often have no U.S. credit, U.S. income or U.S.-based guarantor. Operators fill the gap with a few substitutes: third-party lease guarantee products the student pays for, prepaid rent or a larger deposit where state law allows, underwriting on foreign bank and scholarship documents, or university-backed arrangements. Each one changes who you collect from. A guarantee product usually means a claim to the provider, not a collection account against a parent. Prepaid rent means there may be nothing to collect at all.

Hard numbers on guarantor recovery are scarce. NMHC's 2025 student housing benchmarking survey covers 16 providers, 719 properties and about 400,000 beds, but its detailed results are paid (NMHC). Yardi Matrix's public student housing reports track preleasing and rent per bed, not guarantor collection rates (Yardi). Track your own: how often a guarantor pays once contacted, and how fast.

What a Guarantor File Needs Before You Place It

  1. The signed guaranty, separate from the lease, showing whether it's a guaranty of payment or of collection, any dollar cap, and whether it covers renewals and holdovers.
  2. The guarantor's application data: legal name, address, phone, email, date of birth and employer.
  3. The lease and the final ledger, with the deposit applied within the state deadline.
  4. The move-out statement as sent, to the tenant and, if your guaranty requires it, to the guarantor.
  5. Any eviction judgment, and whether the guarantor was named in it.
  6. Notice records: any demand to sue from the guarantor, with the date received.
  7. The role of each person, so the collector sends the right validation notices and reports the right ECOA codes.

Key takeaways
  • A co-signer on the lease is usually a co-tenant with joint and several liability; a guarantor signs a separate promise.
  • A guaranty of payment lets you go to the guarantor on default; a guaranty of collection makes you pursue the tenant first.
  • Courts read the operative language, not the title, so draft for "payment and not collection" if you want direct recourse.
  • In Texas and Arizona, a guarantor's written demand that you sue starts a clock that can discharge them.
  • Eviction judgments against the tenant generally don't bind a separate guarantor; Arizona bars joining them at all.
  • Under the FDCPA, a guarantor is a consumer with their own validation notice; a parent who signed nothing is a third party.
  • Report the tenant as ECOA 7 and a contingent guarantor as ECOA 5; use ECOA 2 only for true joint liability.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Can I collect unpaid rent from a parent who didn't sign anything?

    No. Without a signed lease or guaranty, the parent owes nothing. A collector may contact them only for location information, with the tenant's direct consent, or with court permission.

  • Do I have to sue the tenant before going after the guarantor?

    Only if the document is a guaranty of collection, or the guarantor uses a statutory demand to sue in Texas or Arizona. A guaranty of payment lets you pursue the guarantor as soon as the tenant defaults.

  • Is a co-signer liable for the full balance or only half?

    Usually the full balance. Most leases make co-signers and co-tenants jointly and severally liable, so you can collect the whole amount from either one.

  • Is a guarantor liable after the lease renews?

    Only if the guaranty covers renewals. Texas Property Code 92.021 limits a guarantor's liability for renewals unless its written conditions are met, and Arizona courts read continuing guaranties strictly.

  • Can a collection agency report a guarantor to the credit bureaus?

    Yes, if the guarantor is liable and was validated. The guarantor should be reported with Metro 2 ECOA code 5 and gets their own dispute rights.

  • Does the guarantor get a separate validation notice?

    Yes, if the collector is seeking payment from them. Each obligor gets their own notice and their own 30-day dispute period.

  • How long do I have to sue a lease guarantor?

    On a written guaranty, about 4 years in Texas and 6 in Arizona, Utah and Oregon, counted from each missed payment. Shorter limits can apply to claims against the tenant, especially in Oregon.

  • What if an international student's lease used a third-party guarantee product?

    Your claim is usually against the guarantee provider under its terms, not a collection account against a parent. File the claim on time and place only what the provider doesn't cover.
