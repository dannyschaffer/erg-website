Options That Aren't Collection Agencies

Sometimes an agency isn't the right tool at all.

  • Credit reporting you handle yourself: some reporting platforms let a landlord report a former tenant's debt to the credit bureaus for a flat fee per debt. They aren't collection agencies, so you handle calls and payments yourself.
  • Placing from your software: AppFolio's Collections Flow, Buildium's Marketplace, Yardi's Collections API and RealPage OneSite's collections interface all send accounts to partner agencies. The partner sets the price and still needs vetting. Our software guide covers each one.
  • Large or disputed balances: the State Bar of Texas Lawyer Referral & Information Service (800-252-9690) offers a 30-minute consultation for $20 (Texas Judicial Branch).
  • Small balances where you know the address: Texas justice court hears claims up to $20,000, with a $54 base filing fee (Texas State Law Library).

How to Vet Any Collection Agency in Texas

Use these checks on every agency above, including us.

Check the Texas Bond Before Anything Else

Texas doesn't license collection agencies. It requires a bond, and you can verify it in two minutes.

Under Finance Code Section 392.101, a third-party debt collector must file a $10,000 surety bond with the Texas Secretary of State before collecting in Texas (Texas SOS FAQ). The Secretary of State's free bond search shows whether a filing is active, pending cancellation or cancelled.

Search the agency's exact legal entity name, not just the name on its website, and ask for any other names it does business under. A filing that's pending cancellation is a reason to pause before placing accounts. The bond rule applies to third-party collectors. A property owner or manager sending its own demand letters for its own receivables doesn't need one, so the check only matters once you hand accounts to an agency.

The Secretary of State files the bonds but doesn't supervise how agencies behave day to day. A clean bond record is the entry ticket, not proof of good practice. The next tests cover that.

The Agency Should Know What Texas Lets It Collect

A good Texas agency screens your file before the first letter and tells you what it won't collect.

Most collection risk on rent debt comes from collecting something the lease or the statute doesn't support. Texas has several rules that decide what's collectible, and an agency that knows them will ask about each one when it reviews your file. One that takes every balance as given is leaving the risk with you, because the claim is still yours.

Federal Compliance Should Be on Paper, Not Just Promised

Every agency says it's FDCPA compliant. Ask it to show you how.

Texas agencies follow the federal FDCPA and Regulation F on top of Chapter 392. Those rules are specific enough to test. Ask for these four things before you sign:

  • A sample validation notice. Regulation F requires the debt's itemization, current balance and dispute rights in the first communication or within five days of it (12 CFR 1006.34). Check that the itemization date and balance match your ledger.
  • Its call-frequency rule. A collector is presumed compliant if it places no more than seven calls about a debt in seven days and doesn't call again within seven days after a conversation (CFPB). Ask whether the dialer enforces it automatically.
  • Its email and text opt-out. Every electronic message needs a clear, simple way to opt out (12 CFR 1006.6). See our guide to text and email rules.
  • Its record retention. CFPB exam procedures expect compliance records kept for three years after the last collection activity and call recordings for three years from the call.

An agency that hesitates to share these is telling you something. Your residents will see these documents, and so will any regulator who looks at a complaint.

Credit Reporting: Ask How It's Done

Credit reporting helps recovery, but only when the agency handles disputes and corrections properly.

Agencies that report rental debt send it to the bureaus in Metro 2, the industry's standard data format (CDIA). Using the format doesn't make the reporting accurate. The agency is a furnisher under the FCRA and must have written accuracy policies, investigate disputes and correct bad data (12 CFR Part 1022). Before reporting a debt, it must also contact the resident and wait a reasonable period, generally 14 days, for a notice of undeliverability (CFPB).

Ask which bureaus it reports to, the minimum balance and identity data it needs, how it marks a disputed account, and how fast it updates or deletes an account you recall or settle. Inaccurate reporting on a former resident lands back on your property's name.

How Much Does a Collection Agency Charge in Texas?

Rental debt is usually worked on contingency. The fee only matters next to the recovery rate.

The National Apartment Association says multifamily collection agencies generally charge nothing up front and keep about 20% to 40% of what they collect. It puts national recovery on rental debt at 15% to 20% of placed balances, and below 10% in some affordable-housing submarkets (NAA). Our fee guide covers what moves the rate.

The useful number is net recovery: what you keep per dollar placed after the agency's fee. At a 20% gross recovery and a 35% fee, you net 13 cents on every dollar placed. A cheaper agency that recovers less can leave you with less.

Speed and File Quality Matter More Than the Pitch

The two things that most change recovery are under your control, so an agency should push you on both.

NAA reports that an account becomes about 16% less likely to be collected for every 30 days it sits unworked, and that agencies can recover up to three times more when a placement includes full Social Security number, date of birth and driver's license data (NAA). An agency that accepts thin files without asking for more, or doesn't push you to place quickly, is leaving money on the table. Our guide on when to send unpaid rent to collections covers the timing.

Judge results the same way. A blended recovery rate across years of placements hides a lot. Ask for results by placement month, and give a new agency six to twelve months before you compare it with your old one, since NAA notes results take that long to settle.

A Texas Judgment Needs an Asset Plan

Texas generally protects current wages, so the agency should explain how it would recover on a judgment.

In Texas, current wages are generally exempt from garnishment for ordinary consumer debt. Recovery on a judgment usually runs through turnover of nonexempt property (CPRC Ch. 31), bank-account garnishment (Ch. 63) or an abstract of judgment lien on real property (Prop. Code 52.001). An agency that promises wage garnishment on a Texas rent judgment is either confused or overselling. See eviction judgment collection for how this works.

Questions to Ask Any Texas Agency

Use this as your shortlist interview. The answers should come in writing.

  1. What's your exact legal entity name, and is your Texas bond active?
  2. Which charges on this sample file would you decline to collect, and why?
  3. Can I see a sample validation notice and a sample client report?
  4. Do you report to credit bureaus? Which ones, and how do you handle disputes and recalls?
  5. What's every fee you could charge me, including litigation, recalls and integration?
  6. What's your recovery rate on placements like mine, grouped by placement month?
  7. How do you recover on a Texas judgment when wages are exempt?
  8. How do accounts get from my software to you, and how often do you remit?

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Is there a list of licensed collection agencies in Texas?

    Not a license list. Texas uses a bond filing instead, and the Secretary of State runs a free debt collector bond search that shows whether a filing is active, pending cancellation or cancelled. Search the agency's exact legal name, not its marketing name.

  • Can a collection agency add its fee to the former tenant's balance in Texas?

    Only if the lease or applicable law authorizes it. Finance Code Section 392.303 bars collecting any fee, charge or interest the agreement doesn't expressly allow. If your lease is silent, the agency's commission comes out of what it recovers.

  • Should I place Texas balances that are more than four years old?

    Ask the agency to review them charge by charge first. The baseline limitations period is four years under Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 16.004, and recurring rent may accrue period by period. A careful agency will tell you which charges are still suable before it works the file.

  • Do I need a different agency for Houston, Dallas and Austin?

    No. One agency with a current Texas bond can collect statewide. Local rules matter mostly if a balance goes to court, because suits are filed in the county where the property sits. Our city guides for Houston, Dallas and Austin cover those differences.

  • Can I pull accounts back from a collection agency?

    Usually yes, if the contract allows recalls. Ask what the recall fee is, if any, and how fast the agency stops collecting and updates the credit bureaus after you recall or a resident disputes. Get both in writing before you place.

  • How long should I give an agency before judging its results?

    Six to twelve months. NAA notes that recovery on a batch of placements takes that long to settle, so ask for results grouped by placement month rather than one blended rate.
