Should Landlords Hire a Collection Agency for Unpaid Rent?

Should landlords hire a collection agency for unpaid rent, or is it worth handling internally first? The honest answer depends on how far past due the balance is and how much the wait is already costing.

Shawn Juarez, Managing Director, US Collections and Growth · Published July 27, 2026
Quick answer

In most cases, yes — once a balance is more than 60 days past due or a resident has moved out still owing money, a specialist collection agency recovers more of it, faster, and with less compliance exposure than in-house follow-up. The exception is a very small portfolio with only occasional accounts, where a formal placement process may not be worth the overhead yet. Eviction alone averages $3,500–$10,000 once legal fees, lost rent, and turnover costs are counted (Snappt, 2026), which is the real number a landlord is weighing collection against.

Why does unpaid rent become a landlord's problem, not just a tenant's?

Every month a balance sits unresolved, it's compounding lost income, not sitting still.

Unpaid rent doesn't just disappear if it's ignored — it either resolves, gets written off, or turns into an eviction, and all three have a cost attached. Multifamily loan delinquencies reached their highest level since the global financial crisis in the third quarter of 2025, a signal of just how much unresolved resident debt is sitting on operators' books right now (Multifamily Dive, 2025). Landlords filed more than 1.23 million eviction cases across the tracked states and cities in 2025 alone — an average filing rate of 7.9%, or roughly one eviction for every 13 renter households (Eviction Lab via Stateline, 2026).

Eviction is the most expensive way this problem resolves, and it's worth putting a real number on it before deciding whether collection is worth pursuing first.

What are the risks of pursuing unpaid rent in-house?

A landlord chasing a former tenant directly is one misstep away from a compliance complaint, not just an awkward call.

Debt collection communications — who can be contacted, how often, and what has to be disclosed — are governed by the FDCPA and Regulation F, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's federal rulebook for collector conduct (CFPB, Regulation F). An internal team chasing a balance without that discipline built in — inconsistent contact frequency, no validation notice, no documented dispute process — can create exposure even when the underlying debt is completely valid.

The National Apartment Association's own guidance for owners and managers recommends a structured escalation path: reminders, then late fees, then a handoff to a third-party collection agency or legal action if internal efforts don't resolve the balance (NAA, Multifamily Debt Collections Best Practices). Skipping straight from "send another reminder" to "sue" — without the structured middle step — is where most of the avoidable risk sits.

What does a collection agency add that in-house effort can't?

A dedicated agency turns a stalled account into an active recovery pipeline within days, not months.

A collection agency built for property debt specifically — not a generic consumer-debt shop — brings three things most internal teams don't have the infrastructure to run in-house: a licensed, compliance-first process, resident-relationship-safe outreach, and account-type-specific handling for resident balances, move-out debt, eviction costs, and aged accounts. Elite Recovery Group's published data across placements shows an average first payment within 8 days and a 42% right-party contact rate in the first 30 days — speed that's hard to replicate with ad hoc internal follow-up.

This is the same reasoning behind a tenant collection agency model generally: accounts move faster once they're handled by a team whose full-time job is recovery, not leasing or maintenance with collections squeezed in around it.

When should a landlord bring in a collection agency instead of waiting?

The moment a balance crosses 60 days past due, or a resident has already moved out, waiting mostly just shrinks the odds of a good outcome.

Two triggers cover most of the decision: a current resident's balance has passed the point where internal reminders and late fees have had a fair chance to work, or a resident has already moved out and there's no more lease leverage to use. In both cases, the account benefits from a dedicated, structured process — see unpaid rent collection agency for how that placement typically works.

Is a collection agency always the right call?

For a handful of occasional accounts, waiting a cycle might genuinely cost less than a formal placement — at scale, it doesn't.

A landlord with one or two rental units and an occasional late payment may reasonably decide the internal time cost of chasing a small balance is lower than the cost of formal placement. That calculus changes fast at portfolio scale: once a property team is managing dozens or hundreds of units, the aggregate unpaid balance and the compliance risk of inconsistent internal handling both grow faster than the in-house team's capacity to manage them well. That's the point where property management collections stops being optional.

What should a landlord look for before hiring a collection agency?

The agency's compliance record matters as much as its recovery rate.

Recovery rate alone is an incomplete picture — an agency with a strong number and no compliance discipline behind it is a liability wearing a good spreadsheet. Frequently asked questions

  • At what point should a landlord stop handling unpaid rent internally?

    Most property teams escalate once a balance passes 60 days past due, or immediately once a resident has moved out still owing money — at that point the odds of a friendly resolution drop and a structured recovery process outperforms ad hoc follow-up.

  • Is it cheaper to just write off small unpaid rent balances?

    For a handful of small, occasional accounts, writing them off can cost less than the internal time spent chasing them. At portfolio scale, the same math flips — a specialist agency's contingency fee against a real recovery rate outperforms a blanket write-off policy.

  • Can a landlord get in legal trouble for how they collect unpaid rent?

    Yes. Debt collection communications are governed by the FDCPA and Regulation F, and missteps in contact frequency, disclosure, or credit-bureau reporting can create compliance exposure regardless of whether the debt itself is valid.

  • Does hiring a collection agency hurt the relationship with a current resident?

    It depends on the agency. A generic consumer-debt collector can create friction; a portfolio-specific agency built around firm, professional engagement is designed to recover the balance without becoming a reputational liability.

  • What's the difference between a collection agency and pursuing legal action?

    A collection agency pursues recovery through structured outreach and negotiation without going to court, which is faster and cheaper. Legal action (including eviction) is typically reserved for active-lease situations or larger balances where collection efforts alone haven't worked.

  • How fast can a collection agency start recovering a balance?

    Once an account is placed, a compliant agency typically begins outreach within days. Elite Recovery Group's published data shows an average first payment within 8 days of placement.

  • What should a landlord check before hiring a collection agency?

    License number and states covered, whether the process is built for property debt specifically rather than generic consumer debt, how reporting works, and how resident relationships are protected during outreach.
