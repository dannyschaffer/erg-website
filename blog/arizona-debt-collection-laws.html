Arizona Debt Collection Laws: What Landlords & Property Managers Need to Know

Arizona layers licensing rules, a state consumer-fraud statute, voter-approved garnishment limits, and its own security-deposit statute on top of the federal FDCPA. Here's how the pieces fit together for rent and landlord-tenant debt.

Shawn Juarez, Managing Director, US Collections and Growth · Published August 19, 2026
Quick answer

Arizona regulates rent and consumer debt collection through collection-agency licensing under Title 32 (issued by DIFI, not the Corporation Commission), the state's Consumer Fraud Act as a deception backstop to the FDCPA, a 2022 voter-approved measure (Proposition 209) that sharply limits garnishment and raises debtor exemptions, a detailed security-deposit statute, and a 6-year statute of limitations for written contract debt (A.R.S. Title 32, Ch. 9).

Who Actually Licenses Collection Agencies in Arizona

It's not the Corporation Commission. It's a separate financial regulator most people have never heard of.

Arizona's collection-agency framework lives in Title 32, Chapter 9 of the Arizona Revised Statutes (A.R.S. §§ 32-1001 through 32-1057), which defines "collection agency," sets licensing standards, and regulates conduct. The licensing itself is handled by the Arizona Department of Insurance and Financial Institutions (DIFI), not the Arizona Corporation Commission (ACC). Under A.R.S. § 32-1021, anyone wishing to operate a collection agency must file an application with DIFI, supply a financial statement, and post a surety bond scaled to Arizona collection income. The ACC's role is narrower: corporate registration and good-standing certification for entities organized as corporations or LLCs, not agency licensing (DIFI, Licensing Resources).

Arizona's Consumer Protection Layer Beyond the FDCPA

Arizona doesn't have a dedicated "mini-FDCPA," but deceptive collection conduct is still reachable under state law.

The Arizona Consumer Fraud Act (A.R.S. § 44-1521 et seq.) broadly prohibits deception, false promises, misrepresentation, and concealment, and Arizona courts and the Attorney General's office apply it to abusive collection tactics such as inflating balances, misrepresenting authority, or threatening arrest. The AG's consumer guidance is explicit that collectors must comply with the federal FDCPA and may not misrepresent garnishment, threaten arrest, or use false or misleading statements (Arizona AG, Collections and Debt Settlement). In practice, that gives Arizona residents a state-law claim running in parallel with the federal FDCPA, not a replacement for it.

How Proposition 209 Changed Garnishment and Exemptions

Arizona voters made judgment collection considerably harder in 2022, and the changes are still catching collectors off guard.

Arizona voters approved Proposition 209, the Predatory Debt Collection Protection Act, in 2022. It cut wage garnishment on most non-support consumer judgments to the lesser of 10% of disposable earnings or the amount exceeding 60 times the highest applicable minimum wage, down from a prior 25% cap. It also raised exemption amounts for homesteads, vehicles, household goods, and bank accounts, with annual cost-of-living adjustments starting in 2024, and capped interest on medical debt at 3% per year (Arizona Courts, Proposition 209).

Key Arizona limits after Proposition 209
Protection Current limit
Wage garnishment Lesser of 10% of disposable earnings or amount over 60x highest minimum wage
Bank-account exemption $5,000 per institution, CPI-adjusted annually
Homestead exemption $400,000, indexed for inflation
Medical debt interest Capped at 3% per year

For many residents, these exemptions mean a substantial share of wages, bank balances, and home equity is statutorily protected from judgment collection, which directly shapes what a lawful post-judgment enforcement strategy in Arizona actually looks like. For how that plays out once a court judgment is already in hand, see Eviction Judgment Collection: Recovering What the Court Didn't.

Arizona's Security Deposit and Itemization Rules

The deposit clock is short, and missing it can forfeit a landlord's right to withhold anything.

Arizona's residential security-deposit rules sit in the Arizona Residential Landlord and Tenant Act at A.R.S. § 33-1321. A landlord may not demand a deposit larger than one and one-half months' rent. Within 14 days, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays, after the tenancy ends, possession is delivered, and the tenant makes a demand, the landlord must send an itemized list of deductions and any balance due. If the tenant doesn't dispute the deductions within 60 days of that itemized list being mailed, the amount is deemed valid. A landlord who wrongfully withholds a deposit can be liable for the amount withheld plus damages of up to twice that amount under § 33-1321(E).

How Long a Landlord Has to Sue Over Unpaid Rent

Arizona's statute of limitations for debt on a written contract, which covers most written leases, is six years under A.R.S. § 12-548. Oral contracts and open accounts carry a shorter, three-year period under § 12-543. Both are accrual-based, running from the point of default rather than whenever the debt is later discovered. For how Arizona's timeline compares to Oregon, Texas, Utah, and Washington, and what a payment or written acknowledgment does to the clock, see Statute of Limitations on Rent Debt: A State-by-State Guide.

Credit Reporting Runs on the Federal Clock, Not a State One

Arizona doesn't have its own statute governing how long a debt can stay on a credit report. Residents rely on the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act instead, along with the Arizona Attorney General's general credit-reporting guidance covering dispute rights and free annual reports (Arizona AG, Credit Reporting & Credit Repair). Deceptive reporting tactics, such as knowingly furnishing inaccurate data, can still be reached under the state Consumer Fraud Act even without a dedicated reporting statute.

Key takeaways
  • Collection agencies are licensed by DIFI, not the Arizona Corporation Commission, under A.R.S. Title 32, Ch. 9.
  • The Arizona Consumer Fraud Act backstops the FDCPA for deceptive or abusive collection conduct.
  • Proposition 209 (2022) cut wage garnishment to 10% of disposable earnings (or less), raised exemption amounts, and capped medical debt interest at 3%.
  • Security deposits are capped at 1.5 months' rent; landlords have 14 business days to itemize deductions after a tenant's demand, and the tenant has 60 days to dispute.
  • The statute of limitations is 6 years for written contract debt, 3 years for oral/open accounts.
  • There's no separate Arizona credit-reporting statute; the federal FCRA governs.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Which state agency licenses collection agencies in Arizona?

    The Arizona Department of Insurance and Financial Institutions (DIFI), not the Arizona Corporation Commission. DIFI licenses collection agencies under Title 32, Chapter 9 of the Arizona Revised Statutes; the Corporation Commission's role is limited to registering the business entity itself.

  • Does Arizona have its own debt collection law beyond the FDCPA?

    Arizona doesn't have a standalone "mini-FDCPA" statute, but the Arizona Consumer Fraud Act is applied to deceptive or abusive collection conduct, giving residents a state-law claim on top of the federal FDCPA.

  • What did Proposition 209 change for landlords and collectors?

    Proposition 209, approved by Arizona voters in 2022, cut the maximum wage garnishment on most consumer judgments, raised the bank-account and homestead exemption amounts, and capped interest on medical debt at 3% per year.

  • How much security deposit can an Arizona landlord require?

    No more than one and one-half months' rent, under A.R.S. Section 33-1321.

  • How long does an Arizona landlord have to send the itemized deposit statement?

    Fourteen days, excluding weekends and legal holidays, after the tenancy ends, possession is delivered, and the tenant makes a demand. The resident then has 60 days to dispute the deductions before the amount is deemed valid.

  • What is the statute of limitations on rent debt in Arizona?

    Six years for debt evidenced by a written contract, which covers most written leases, under A.R.S. Section 12-548. Oral or open-account debt has a shorter, three-year period under Section 12-543.
