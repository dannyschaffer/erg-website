Options That Aren't Collection Agencies

Sometimes an agency isn't the right tool at all.

  • Credit reporting you handle yourself: some reporting platforms let a landlord report a former tenant's debt to the credit bureaus for a flat fee per debt. They aren't collection agencies, so you handle calls and payments yourself.
  • Placing from your software: AppFolio's Collections Flow, Buildium's Marketplace, Yardi's Collections API and RealPage OneSite's collections interface all send accounts to partner agencies. The partner sets the price and still needs vetting. Our software guide covers each one.
  • Large or disputed balances: the Maricopa County Bar (602-257-4434, $50 for 30 minutes) and Pima County Bar (520-623-4625, $35) run referral services; the State Bar of Arizona doesn't make referrals (State Bar of Arizona).
  • Small balances where you know the address: Arizona small claims now reaches $5,000 (up from $3,500 in 2025), and justice court civil cases reach $10,000 (Arizona Judicial Branch).

How to Vet Any Collection Agency in Arizona

Use these checks on every agency above, including us.

Check the Arizona License First

Arizona licenses collection agencies, and the license comes from DIFI, not the Corporation Commission.

Anyone who collects or solicits claims owed to someone else in Arizona needs a collection agency license under A.R.S. Title 32, Chapter 9. The Arizona Department of Insurance and Financial Institutions issues it, and applications go through NMLS with a surety bond and a financial statement (Arizona DIFI). The Corporation Commission only registers the business entity, so a good-standing certificate from the ACC doesn't show the agency can collect.

Ask for the agency's exact legal entity name, any other names it does business under and its NMLS ID, then look it up on NMLS Consumer Access. If the name on the license doesn't match the name on the contract, ask why before you place anything.

The Agency Should Know What Arizona Lets It Collect

A good Arizona agency screens your file before the first letter and tells you what it won't collect.

Most collection risk on rent debt comes from collecting something the lease or the statute doesn't support. Arizona has no standalone state version of the FDCPA, but its Consumer Fraud Act (A.R.S. 44-1521 and following) reaches deceptive collection conduct, and the Attorney General's guidance applies it to inflated balances and misstatements (Arizona AG). An agency that takes every balance as given leaves that risk with you.

Federal Compliance Should Be on Paper, Not Just Promised

Every agency says it's FDCPA compliant. Ask it to show you how.

Arizona agencies follow the federal FDCPA and Regulation F. Those rules are specific enough to test. Ask for these four things before you sign:

  • A sample validation notice. Regulation F requires the debt's itemization, current balance and dispute rights in the first communication or within five days of it (12 CFR 1006.34). Check that the itemization date and balance match your ledger.
  • Its call-frequency rule. A collector is presumed compliant if it places no more than seven calls about a debt in seven days and doesn't call again within seven days after a conversation (CFPB). Arizona doesn't observe daylight saving time, so ask how the dialer handles calling hours for residents who moved out of state.
  • Its email and text opt-out. Every electronic message needs a clear, simple way to opt out (12 CFR 1006.6).
  • Its record retention. CFPB exam procedures expect compliance records kept for three years after the last collection activity and call recordings for three years from the call.

Credit Reporting: Ask How It's Done

Arizona has no credit-reporting statute of its own, so federal rules set the standard.

Agencies that report rental debt send it to the bureaus in Metro 2, the industry's standard data format (CDIA). The agency is a furnisher under the FCRA and must have written accuracy policies, investigate disputes and correct bad data (12 CFR Part 1022). Before reporting, it must contact the resident and wait a reasonable period, generally 14 days, for a notice of undeliverability (CFPB).

Ask which bureaus it reports to, the minimum balance and identity data it needs, how it marks a disputed account, and how fast it updates or deletes an account you recall or settle.

How Much Does a Collection Agency Charge in Arizona?

Rental debt is usually worked on contingency. The fee only matters next to the recovery rate.

The National Apartment Association says multifamily collection agencies generally charge nothing up front and keep about 20% to 40% of what they collect. It puts national recovery on rental debt at 15% to 20% of placed balances, and below 10% in some affordable-housing submarkets (NAA). Our fee guide covers what moves the rate.

The useful number is net recovery: what you keep per dollar placed after the agency's fee. At a 20% gross recovery and a 35% fee, you net 13 cents on every dollar placed.

Prop 209 Changed What a Judgment Is Worth

Arizona voters cut garnishment and raised exemptions in 2022. An agency's recovery plan should reflect that.

Proposition 209 capped wage garnishment on most consumer judgments at the lesser of 10% of disposable earnings or the amount above 60 times the highest applicable minimum wage, down from 25%. It also raised exemptions, including $5,000 per bank account institution, adjusted each year for inflation (Arizona Courts). A judgment still has value, but it collects more slowly. Ask the agency when it recommends suing and what it expects to recover afterward. One that pitches fast garnishment on Arizona rent judgments hasn't caught up. See eviction judgment collection for the details.

Speed and File Quality Matter More Than the Pitch

With judgments harder to collect, recovery before court matters more in Arizona, and that starts with your placement.

NAA reports that an account becomes about 16% less likely to be collected for every 30 days it sits unworked, and that agencies can recover up to three times more when a placement includes full Social Security number, date of birth and driver's license data (NAA). Arizona's seasonal renters make the first point sharper: winter visitors leave in spring, and a balance placed in May is harder to reach than one placed in March. Our guide on when to send unpaid rent to collections covers the timing.

Judge results by placement month, and give a new agency six to twelve months before you compare it with your old one.

Questions to Ask Any Arizona Agency

Use this as your shortlist interview. The answers should come in writing.

  1. What's your exact legal entity name and NMLS ID, and is your Arizona license current?
  2. Which charges on this sample file would you decline to collect, and why?
  3. How do you handle a balance where the deposit itemization was late or disputed?
  4. Can I see a sample validation notice and a sample client report?
  5. Do you report to credit bureaus? Which ones, and how do you handle disputes and recalls?
  6. What's every fee you could charge me, including litigation, recalls and integration?
  7. When do you recommend suing, and what do you expect to recover after Prop 209?
  8. How do accounts get from my software to you, and how often do you remit?

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Does the Arizona Corporation Commission license collection agencies?

    No. The Corporation Commission only registers the business entity. Collection agency licenses come from the Arizona Department of Insurance and Financial Institutions under A.R.S. Title 32, Chapter 9, and applications run through NMLS.

  • How long can an Arizona rent balance be collected?

    For debt on a written contract, which covers most written leases, the limitations period is six years under A.R.S. 12-548. Oral contracts and open accounts have three years under 12-543. The clock runs from default, so a careful agency will check charge dates before it works an older file.

  • What happens if a former tenant disputes the deposit deductions?

    Under A.R.S. 33-1321, a tenant who doesn't dispute the itemized deductions within 60 days of the list being mailed is treated as accepting them. A dispute inside that window should go back to you for review before the agency keeps collecting that part of the balance.

  • Is an Arizona-based agency better than a national one?

    Not automatically. What matters is a current Arizona license, a process built for former-resident debt and knowledge of Arizona's deposit, limitations and post-Prop 209 rules. A local agency can be easier to reach and more familiar with Maricopa and Pima County courts, but check the same things either way.

  • Can I pull accounts back from a collection agency?

    Usually yes, if the contract allows recalls. Ask what the recall fee is, if any, and how fast the agency stops collecting and updates the credit bureaus after you recall or a resident disputes. Get both in writing before you place.

  • How long should I give an agency before judging its results?

    Six to twelve months. NAA notes that recovery on a batch of placements takes that long to settle, so ask for results grouped by placement month rather than one blended rate.
