What Texas Property Managers Should Know Before Hiring a Collection Agency

Texas is not a state where a property manager can treat rent recovery as generic collections work. The state uses a bond filing instead of a general collection-agency license, overlays its own debt-collection rules on federal law, and sharply narrows what post-judgment enforcement usually looks like.

Shawn Juarez, Managing Director, US Collections and Growth · LinkedIn · Published August 10, 2026 · 12 min read
Quick answer

Before placing Texas rent debt with a third-party agency, confirm that the collector has the $10,000 surety bond Texas requires for third-party debt collectors and credit bureaus, understand that the Texas Debt Collection Act sits on top of the FDCPA and Regulation F, review older charges against the four-year limitations framework in Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 16.004, make sure the move-out balance is backed by compliant deposit accounting under Property Code Chapter 92, and expect recovery on a Texas judgment to focus on nonexempt property, qualifying bank-account garnishment, and judgment liens rather than current wages.

Texas uses a bond filing, not a general collection-agency license.

Texas does not require a general statewide collection-agency license for this work. What it does require is narrower and still important: according to the Texas Secretary of State's debt-collector FAQ, third-party debt collectors and credit bureaus must file a $10,000 surety bond with the Secretary of State before engaging in debt collection. The same FAQ points back to Finance Code Chapter 392, where Section 392.101 sets the bond rule and ties it to Texas debt-collection compliance.

That distinction matters because property managers often ask the wrong first question. In Texas, the right opening check is not "show me your collection-agency license." It is "show me your Texas bond filing, and let me verify it through the Secretary of State." The Secretary of State also states that its office is the filing officer for those bonds, not the regulator of day-to-day collection practices, which is another reason a Texas placement review has to cover both bond status and operating discipline.

The bond point should also stay in scope. It applies to third-party collectors and credit bureaus. A property owner or manager collecting its own receivable is not making a third-party bond filing simply because it sends its own statements or demand letters. If the account is being handled by a third-party collection agency for property managers, the bond question comes back immediately.

Texas overlays the TDCA on federal rent-collection rules.

Once a Texas property manager uses a third-party agency, federal law still matters, including the FDCPA and Regulation F framework. Texas then adds another layer through Chapter 392 of the Finance Code. That matters on the content side because Section 392.303 prohibits collecting a fee, charge, or interest unless the lease or applicable law authorizes it, and it matters on the remedy side because Sections 392.403 and 392.404 create private-action and deceptive-trade-practices exposure for violations.

The state-law overlap is why Texas account setup cannot be reduced to a balance file and a forwarding agreement. A clean placement package should already line up the signed lease, the ledger, any active-lease tenant balance documentation, and any move-out debt itemization so the collector is not tempted to add amounts the contract does not support. If the lease language around late fees, charges, or interest is weak, that is a placement issue, not just a later litigation issue.

Texas case law also cautions against casually stacking extra accrual on top of the base rent claim. In Samson Exploration, LLC v. Bordages, the Supreme Court of Texas held in a different contract setting that monthly-due late-charge language did not by itself create compound interest. For property managers, the takeaway is practical: do not assume recurring late-charge language automatically compounds or that a collector can build interest on interest without clear contractual support.

Older rent balances need charge-level limitations review, not a single move-out date shortcut.

The baseline Texas limitations rule for debt and written-contract claims is four years under Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 16.004. That is the right starting point for unpaid rent, but it is not always enough to stop the analysis there.

Texas case law recognizes that when an agreement calls for recurring periodic payments, the claim may accrue period by period. In Hooks v. Samson Lone Star, the Supreme Court of Texas said that if an agreement calls for periodic payments during the course of the contract, a cause of action for those payments may arise at the end of each period. That opinion arose in an oil-and-gas royalty dispute, not a residential rent case, so it should not be over-read. It does, however, support a cautious collections practice: review older Texas rent charges line by line instead of treating every delinquency as one all-or-nothing claim measured only from move-out.

That is one reason what happens when unpaid rent goes to collections in Texas depends heavily on account age and documentation quality. A collector may still have a strong path on more recent months even when much older charges need to be carved out. For portfolio teams managing scale, that makes early placement through a property management collections workflow more valuable than waiting until every internal touch has failed.

Chapter 92 deposit timing often determines whether the move-out balance is defensible.

Texas Property Code Chapter 92 is not a collection statute, but it often determines whether a move-out debt file is stable enough to place. Under Property Code Chapter 92, the landlord generally must refund the security deposit or provide an itemized deduction statement within 30 days after the tenant surrenders the premises. For a collector, that timing matters because the statement is often the bridge between the lease ledger and the final claim.

If the deposit file is sloppy, missing, or late, the problem usually does not improve when a third party touches it. It becomes harder to support the account, easier for the resident to dispute the balance, and riskier to report or collect through any unpaid rent collection agency or collection agency for landlords. Texas properties that want cleaner recoveries usually get there by fixing the move-out paper trail before placement, not by asking the collector to rehabilitate it later.

Texas judgment enforcement usually turns to nonexempt property rather than current wages.

Texas is not friendly to the common assumption that a rent judgment can simply be garnished from payroll. In Texas, current wages are generally exempt from garnishment for ordinary consumer debt, so a rent judgment usually has to be enforced another way. That does not mean the judgment is worthless. It means the recovery path shifts.

Three Texas tools matter most here. First, Civil Practice and Remedies Code Chapter 31 provides turnover relief aimed at nonexempt property. Second, Chapter 63 governs garnishment procedure, which can matter for bank accounts or other debts owed to the judgment debtor, subject to exemptions and source-of-funds defenses. Third, Property Code Section 52.001 addresses the abstract-of-judgment lien route for qualifying nonexempt real property in the county where the abstract is recorded.

That is why eviction judgment collection in Texas looks more asset-focused than payroll-focused. If the debtor has reachable nonexempt property or a bank relationship that can support post-judgment process, the judgment may still be worth real money. If not, the property manager needs to price the account like a long-tail asset-recovery file, not like a simple wage-garnishment file from another state.

A Texas agency review should test proof, bond status, and recovery path before launch.

For Texas portfolios, the best diligence question is not whether the collector sounds national. It is whether the collector can show the bond filing Texas expects of third-party collectors, work inside the federal compliance rules and Chapter 392 at the same time, and tell you what the likely Texas recovery path is before the first letter ever goes out.

Before placing accounts in Texas, a property manager should confirm the collector's bond filing with the Secretary of State, make sure the lease supports the amounts being claimed, and check that the recovery plan fits Texas enforcement limits. A capable Texas partner will have that bond on file and be able to walk you through the recovery path before the first letter goes out. The same discipline applies whether you are evaluating a specialist best-fit unpaid-rent agency, deciding whether to outsource rent collections, or comparing a broad rental debt collection platform to a Texas-specific placement plan.

Key takeaways
  • Texas does not use a general collection-agency license for this work, but it does require a $10,000 bond filing for third-party debt collectors and credit bureaus through the Secretary of State.
  • The Texas Debt Collection Act adds state-specific conduct and remedy exposure on top of the FDCPA and Regulation F.
  • The four-year limitations period in Section 16.004 is the baseline, but recurring payment obligations may need charge-level review under Hooks.
  • Texas Supreme Court authority in Samson Exploration, LLC v. Bordages cautions against assuming monthly late-charge language creates compound interest.
  • Property Code Chapter 92 deposit timing and itemization often determine whether the move-out balance is defensible enough to place.
  • Texas judgments usually turn to nonexempt property, bank-account process, and judgment liens rather than current wages, using turnover, garnishment, and abstract-of-judgment lien tools where appropriate.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Does Texas require a collection-agency license for third-party rent collection?

    No. Texas does not impose a general collection-agency license for this work, but third-party debt collectors and credit bureaus must file a $10,000 surety bond with the Secretary of State before engaging in debt collection under Finance Code Section 392.101.

  • Does a property manager collecting its own rent need the Texas surety bond?

    Generally no. The Secretary of State guidance and Finance Code bond provision are directed to third-party debt collectors and credit bureaus, not a property owner or manager collecting its own account. The conduct rules in Chapter 392 still matter because Texas applies parts of the TDCA more broadly than the federal FDCPA.

  • Can a Texas collector add late fees, collection fees, or interest to unpaid rent?

    Only if the lease or applicable law authorizes them. Finance Code Section 392.303 bars collecting a charge, fee, or interest unless it is expressly authorized by the agreement creating the obligation or legally chargeable to the consumer.

  • How long does a Texas landlord usually have to sue for unpaid rent?

    The baseline limitations period for debt and written-contract claims is four years under Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 16.004. For obligations structured as recurring periodic payments, Texas case law recognizes that a cause of action for those payments may arise at the end of each period, so older charges should be reviewed line by line rather than treated as one single move-out-date claim.

  • Does Texas allow wage garnishment on a residential rent judgment?

    Usually no. Current wages are generally exempt from garnishment for ordinary consumer debts, so post-judgment recovery usually turns to nonexempt property, qualifying bank-account garnishment, and judgment liens instead.

  • What is the 30-day security-deposit rule and why does it matter for collections?

    Texas Property Code Chapter 92 generally requires the landlord to refund the security deposit or provide an itemized deduction statement within 30 days after surrender. For collections, that accounting often becomes the document trail supporting the move-out balance later placed with a third-party agency.

  • Can a Texas judgment still reach a debtor's bank account or real property?

    Potentially yes. Texas turnover procedures target nonexempt property, post-judgment garnishment can reach some debts or bank accounts subject to exemptions, and an abstract of judgment can create a lien on qualifying nonexempt real property in the county where it is recorded.

  • How can a property manager confirm a collector is set up to work accounts in Texas?

    Check three things before placing accounts: the collector's bond filing with the Texas Secretary of State, that the lease supports the amounts being claimed, and that the recovery plan fits Texas enforcement limits. A collector active in Texas will have the bond on file and be able to explain the recovery path up front.
