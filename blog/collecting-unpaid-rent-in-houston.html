Collecting Unpaid Rent in Houston, TX: A Property Manager's Guide

Texas Property Code and Finance Code set the statewide floor, covered in our Texas rent collection law guide. This is what's materially different in Houston: Harris County's Justice of the Peace court system, its county-funded tenant-assistance infrastructure, and current Houston multifamily conditions, set against our companion piece on Dallas.

Shawn Juarez, Managing Director, US Collections and Growth · Published September 16, 2026
Quick answer

Houston evictions are filed in the Harris County Justice of the Peace court for the precinct where the property sits, confirmed by address lookup rather than assumed by neighborhood. Median time from filing to judgment was 24 days in 2024, with a realistic notice-to-lockout timeline of 4 to 6 weeks. Harris County funds a structured eviction-diversion and legal-aid infrastructure that residents in high-filing precincts increasingly use, which can extend negotiations even though it doesn't change the underlying statute. Which Court Hears Houston Eviction Cases

Harris County Justice of the Peace courts, not County Civil Court at Law, handle initial eviction filings, and Houston proper is split across multiple precincts.

Eviction suits must be filed in the Justice of the Peace court for the precinct where the rental property is located; filing in the wrong precinct leads to dismissal for improper venue. Harris County has 8 precincts with 16 JP courts, two "places" per precinct, and Houston-proper properties fall across several of them depending on address: Precinct 1 covers central and north-central Houston, Precinct 4 covers northwest Houston toward Cypress and the Humble fringe, Precinct 5 covers west and southwest Houston (Chimney Rock, Clay Road), and Precinct 7 covers south and southeast Houston (Cullen, Griggs), with Precincts 2, 3, and 8 covering parts of greater Houston metro including Pasadena, Baytown, and Clear Lake. Because boundaries don't track neighborhoods cleanly, always confirm the exact precinct through the Harris County JP precinct locator before drafting a petition.

Filing Process and Typical Timeline

Harris County's own statistics put the median case at 24 days from filing to judgment, but the process is faster in mechanics than the volume of filings might suggest.

After the required notice to vacate expires, the landlord files the petition in the correct precinct and pays filing and service fees. A citation is issued and served by the sheriff or constable, with a trial date typically set 10 to 21 days after filing. If the landlord prevails, judgment for possession is entered, along with any rent due up to $20,000. A 5-day appeal window follows the signed judgment, and a writ of possession cannot issue before day 6, absent a possession bond; the constable then executes the writ, posting a 24-hour warning before removing occupants or property. Harris County's own 2024 data puts the median time from filing to judgment at 24 days, and a realistic end-to-end timeline from notice to lockout in an uncontested case runs about 4 to 6 weeks, sometimes 3 to 4 under accelerated procedures where the tenant doesn't respond. Texas SB 38, effective 2026, added summary-disposition options that can produce judgments as early as day 10 after filing if no genuine dispute is raised, applied within the same precinct-venue framework.

2024 filings totaled 76,321 across the county, roughly 1 in 10 renter households, down about 5% from 2023's 80,110 but still around 20% above pre-pandemic averages. Over half of all filings concentrate in Precincts 4 and 5, and local reporting in early 2025 described more than 500 cases scheduled in a single day in Precinct 5, Place 1, with tenants waiting for hours, indicating real docket strain in the highest-volume courts even though the statutory timeline is generally adhered to.

Harris County's Tenant-Protection and Diversion Infrastructure

Texas preempts most local rent-control ordinances, but Harris County has built a programmatic and funding-based layer that shapes how delinquent accounts actually play out.

Properties that receive Harris County funds must include lease addenda with just-cause eviction protections and timely information on rental assistance and legal aid; this doesn't apply to every Houston rental, but it binds county-subsidized assets and shapes negotiations with affordable-housing operators. The county's Eviction Defense Program funds free legal advice and representation for qualifying low-income residents (income at or below 200% of the federal poverty guideline) at risk of eviction, which increases the likelihood of tenant appearance and negotiated resolutions on county-funded or targeted properties. In some Harris JP courts, dedicated eviction-diversion facilitators contact tenants before the hearing to explain the process and connect them to legal aid or rental assistance; one court reported a 44% drop in default judgments after the initiative began. The Houston Eviction Advocacy Center, launched in 2025, extends free legal assistance in the highest-filing precincts, notably 2 and 5. For a landlord, especially in the high-volume precincts, the practical implication is more tenant outreach, more mediation offers, and higher appearance rates than in many Texas counties, which can lengthen negotiations but also reduce post-judgment turnover risk when payment plans are honored.

Two recent disaster cycles still color local policy: Hurricane Harvey's 2017 displacement still informs Harris County's legal-aid and just-cause funding choices, and Texas Housers' 2024 eviction analysis explicitly notes filing dips coinciding with the May 2024 derecho and Hurricane Beryl, showing that recent storm cycles continue to modulate filing volume and negotiating posture in the county.

Enforcement Patterns and What's Sparse

Unlike Phoenix's active AG suit, public Houston-specific enforcement data on private rent-debt collection is thin, and the more useful local context is the county's diversion infrastructure rather than any AG or BBB enforcement pattern.

Statewide Texas AG press releases discuss debt-collection enforcement broadly but don't single out Houston property managers or rent-debt collectors with recurring, named actions in the current window, and no aggregated, Houston-specific BBB dataset on property-manager debt collection was found in public summaries; BBB profiles are company-specific rather than metro-aggregated. For a Houston-focused article, the safer, citable framing is Harris County's eviction-diversion and legal-aid environment, which materially affects how a collections strategy plays out on the ground, rather than an unverified enforcement trend.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Which court hears eviction cases in Houston?

    Harris County Justice of the Peace courts, not Harris County Civil Court at Law, hear initial eviction filings. Harris County has 8 precincts with 16 JP courts, and Houston-proper addresses fall across several of them depending on location, so the correct precinct must be confirmed by address using the county's JP locator before filing. Filing in the wrong precinct leads to dismissal for improper venue.

  • How long does an eviction take in Harris County?

    The 2024 median time from filing to judgment was 24 days. A realistic end-to-end timeline from notice to lockout in a straightforward, uncontested case runs about 4 to 6 weeks: a 3-day notice, 10 to 21 days to hearing, a 5-day appeal window, then a writ issued on day 6 or later and executed within several business days. Texas SB 38, effective 2026, introduced summary-disposition options that can produce judgments as early as day 10 after filing when no genuine dispute is raised.

  • Does Houston or Harris County have tenant protections beyond Texas state law?

    Texas preempts most local rent-control-style ordinances, but Harris County layers programmatic protections on top of state law for county-funded properties: mandatory just-cause and rental-assistance lease addenda, a county-funded Eviction Defense Program offering free legal representation for residents at or below 200% of the federal poverty guideline, court-based eviction diversion facilitators, and the Houston Eviction Advocacy Center, launched in 2025 to serve the highest-filing precincts. These create a more structured tenant-outreach environment than in many Texas counties, which can extend negotiations even though it doesn't change the underlying statute.

  • How does Houston's eviction volume compare to Dallas's?

    Harris County's absolute filing volume is higher: 76,321 evictions filed in 2024 versus 50,117 in Dallas County, though Dallas's total was a 25-year high for that county. Harris County reporting shows more acute single-court backlog, including over 500 cases scheduled in one day in a single Precinct 5 court, while Dallas coverage emphasizes its record annual total rather than single-day docket overload. Median time from filing to judgment in Harris County was 24 days in 2024, suggesting the statutory timeline holds up despite the volume.

  • Is there Houston-specific data on rent delinquency?

    Not at the tenant level. Major providers (Matthews, Yardi Matrix, NorthMarq, CoStar) publish Houston vacancy and rent growth but not a metro-wide tenant delinquency percentage. The closest named, MSA-specific figure is Trepp's 1.90% 30-plus-day delinquency rate for the Houston-The Woodlands-Pasadena MSA, which reflects securitized loan performance, not individual tenant arrears, and shouldn't be presented as a tenant-level statistic.

  • What's the current Houston multifamily market condition?

    Oversupplied. Multiple 2025 reports show vacancy at 11 to 12% or higher, a 20-year high in one quarter, with negative year-over-year rent growth through most of 2025, the first negative stretch in more than a decade by some measures. A 2026 outlook from NorthMarq projects vacancy easing roughly 50 basis points and rent growth rebounding toward 2024's pace as new supply decelerates.
