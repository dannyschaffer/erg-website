Compliance and Regulation F

The Regulation F Validation Notice, Explained

When you place a former resident's balance with a collection agency, the first thing that resident receives is a validation notice. It is the most heavily specified document in debt collection, it is your agency's obligation rather than yours, and it cannot be produced without data only you hold. Here is what has to be on it and what your ledger has to supply.

Shawn Juarez, Managing Director, US Collections and Growth · Published September 9, 2026
Quick answer

A Regulation F validation notice is the written or electronic notice a debt collector must provide under 12 CFR 1006.34, either in its initial communication with the consumer or within five days of it. It must carry nine specific pieces of information about the debt, including the itemization date, the amount owed on that date, and an itemization of interest, fees, payments, and credits since then, with no field left blank. It must also state the date the thirty-day validation period ends. The obligation runs to the collection agency, not to the landlord, but the notice cannot be produced without creditor-supplied ledger data.

What the Regulation F Validation Notice Is, and Who Has to Send It

The validation notice is the written or electronic notice that carries the validation information required by 12 CFR 1006.34, and the obligation to send it falls on the collection agency, not on you.

Regulation F took full effect on November 30, 2021, and it binds third-party debt collectors rather than landlords collecting their own rent in their own name. Once you place a former resident's balance with an agency, that agency has three compliant paths under 1006.34(a)(1): include the validation information in its initial communication, send the notice within five days of that initial communication, or provide the information orally in the initial communication. The CFPB reads those five days as five calendar days. The one exception is a consumer who pays the debt before the notice would have been due, under 1006.34(a)(2). The CFPB has also been explicit that unpaid residential rent falls within the FDCPA's definition of debt, because it is an obligation arising out of a transaction for personal, family, or household purposes, so rental placements are squarely inside this regime. If the underlying rules are new to you, start with our explainer on what Regulation F is, then come back here for the notice itself.

The Nine Things That Must Appear on the Notice

Section 1006.34(c)(2) lists nine required content elements, and the rule that trips up rental placements most often is that none of them may be left blank.

A notice that omits a required field is non-compliant even when the field is genuinely empty. Comment 1006.34(c)(2)(viii)-1 is direct about it: a collector must include fields for all of these items even if nothing has been assessed or applied since the itemization date, and may show a value of "0" or "none," but may not leave a required field blank. For a rental balance with no interest ever charged, that means the notice must still carry an interest line reading zero. The practical consequence for you is that your ledger needs explicit zero rows rather than missing ones.

On top of those, 1006.34(c)(1) carries the debt-collector disclosure, 1006.34(c)(3) carries the consumer-protection statements with the validation period end date, and 1006.34(c)(4) requires a segregated response block at the bottom of the notice under the headings "How do you want to respond?" and "Check all that apply:". A collector that uses Model Form B-1 from Appendix B gets a safe harbor for the content and format requirements under 1006.34(d)(2), and a second safe harbor against the overshadowing prohibition under 1006.38(b)(2). Asking your agency for a specimen notice benchmarked against B-1 is the fastest way to judge whether it is running a serious compliance operation.

Two Clocks Run at Once: Five Days to Send, Thirty Days to Dispute

The five-day rule is the collector's deadline to deliver the notice; the thirty-day validation period is the tenant's window to dispute. They are separate, and neither one freezes collection by itself.

This is the single most common misreading of Regulation F. The five days are not a five-day prohibition on collecting, and the thirty days are not an automatic pause. Under FDCPA section 1692g(b), collection activities and communications that do not otherwise violate the law may continue during the validation period, unless the consumer has disputed in writing or asked for the original creditor's name and address. What a collector may not do during that window is defined by 1006.38(b)(1): no collection activity that overshadows or is inconsistent with the disclosure of the tenant's dispute rights. Running a parallel eviction demand alongside the agency's letters during the validation period is exactly the kind of conduct that creates overshadowing exposure. The seven-calls-in-seven-days presumption is also live throughout.

When Does the Validation Period Actually End?

Five days for assumed receipt, excluding weekends and federal holidays, then thirty calendar days. Reg F requires the notice to state that end date as a date certain.

Section 1006.34(b)(5) is the provision that turned an open-ended statutory standard into arithmetic. The FDCPA spoke of thirty days after receipt of the notice, which nobody could calculate reliably. Reg F lets the collector assume receipt on any date at least five days after it provides the notice, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and the federal holidays listed in 5 U.S.C. 6103(a), and then runs thirty calendar days from there. The CFPB's own worked example: a notice mailed on Monday April 1 is assumed received on Monday April 8, the validation period ends Wednesday May 8, and contact resuming on Thursday May 9 is compliant. A read receipt arriving earlier does not force a recalculation. Undeliverable mail does reset it: a notice returned on July 17 and re-mailed on July 20 produces a new assumed receipt of July 27 and a new end date of August 27. Ask your agency to show you its written procedure for this calculation, including re-issuance after a bounce, because CFPB examiners have cited collectors for exactly that failure.

What Happens When a Tenant Disputes

A written dispute inside the validation period forces the agency to cease collection until it sends verification of the debt or a copy of a judgment.

The mechanism sits in 1006.38(d)(2). On receipt of a written dispute submitted within the validation period, the collector must stop collecting the debt, or the disputed portion of it, until it sends the consumer verification or a copy of a judgment. Where the collector reasonably determines a dispute is duplicative of an earlier one, it can instead notify the consumer in writing that the dispute is duplicative, give a brief statement of reasons, and refer back to its earlier response. A written request for the original creditor's name and address triggers the same cease-collection duty until that information is sent. Note that the trigger is the consumer's submission date, not the collector's receipt date, which is another workflow detail worth confirming with your agency. Disputes are not a fringe event: the CFPB's FDCPA Annual Report covering 2024 counted 207,800 debt collection complaints, with 45 percent concerning attempts to collect a debt not owed and 31 percent concerning written notification about a debt.

Email and Text Delivery Is the Part You Control

Your agency can only email or text a former resident if the address or number qualifies under 1006.6(d), and one of the qualifying pathways depends entirely on a notice you send before placement.

Section 1006.6(d)(4) gives three routes to a usable email address. The consumer used it to communicate with the collector about the debt, or gave direct consent, or the creditor route applies. That third route, 1006.6(d)(4)(ii), is the one a property manager can actually enable, and it has five conditions: you obtained the address from the resident, you used it to communicate about the account without being asked to stop, you sent a clear written notice disclosing that the debt has been or will be transferred and that the collector may use the address, you gave a reasonable opt-out method with a deadline at least 35 days out, and that period expired without an opt-out. Text numbers carry their own conditions under 1006.6(d)(5), including a reassignment check within the past 60 days. Delivery of the notice itself must also meet 1006.42(a)(1), which requires a manner reasonably expected to provide actual notice in a form the consumer can keep. Electronic delivery within the five-day window additionally requires E-SIGN consent under 1006.42(b), while electronic delivery inside the initial communication does not. Our guide to the digital contact rules covers the opt-out mechanics in more detail.

What Your Ledger Has to Supply

Nothing in 1006.34 imposes duties on you directly, but the notice is impossible to produce without creditor-supplied data, and the no-blank-fields rule turns gaps into blockers.

At placement, the agency needs the resident's full legal name, the last known or forwarding mailing address, the property owner's legal entity name as current creditor, the lease or unit reference used as the account number, the itemization date and proof of the statement or invoice that supports it, the balance owed on that date, and an itemized ledger of interest, fees, payments, and credits since then, with explicit zeroes where nothing accrued. One point worth flagging: a collection agency's own statement cannot create a last statement date under Comment 1006.34(b)(3)(i)-1, so your final invoice or deposit-disposition statement is usually the right anchor. Because choosing and defending that anchor date is where most rental placements go wrong, we cover it separately in our guide to the Regulation F itemization date. Lumped charges are a related risk: uniform damage amounts repeated across accounts are precisely the pattern consumer advocates point to when arguing a portfolio deserves scrutiny, so itemize damages properly rather than applying a standard figure.

What Changed in 2024 to 2026, and What Did Not

The federal validation-notice text has not changed since 2021. What moved is enforcement posture and state or municipal overlays.

Sections 1006.34, 1006.38, 1006.42, and Model Form B-1 all carry amendment dates of 2021 and have not been amended since, so everything above is the operative text as of September 2026. Three things did shift. First, CFPB Supervisory Highlights Issue 34, published July 2024, cited collectors for failing to provide validation notices within five days and for failing to re-send after learning a notice was not delivered. Second, in May 2025 the CFPB withdrew roughly seventy guidance documents, including three Regulation F advisory items on medical debt, time-barred debt, and pay-to-pay fees. None of those touched 1006.34, and the regulation, the official interpretations, and the Small Entity Compliance Guide were not withdrawn. Third, with federal enforcement activity reduced, private FDCPA litigation and state or municipal enforcement have become the dominant exposure, and municipal overlays are getting stricter. New York City's SHIELD Rule, now effective January 1, 2027, goes well beyond Reg F with open-ended dispute rights, a three-contact weekly cap, and hard verification deadlines. It does not reach the states we work in, but it is a fair signal of where local rules are heading. Key takeaways
  • The validation notice is the agency's obligation under 12 CFR 1006.34, not the landlord's, and it attaches once you place the account.
  • Three compliant paths: information in the initial communication, a notice within five days of it, or the information given orally in that first contact.
  • Nine content elements are required and none may be blank; a rental balance with no interest still needs an interest line showing zero.
  • The validation period ends five days after sending, excluding weekends and federal holidays, plus thirty calendar days, and the notice must state that date.
  • Neither clock freezes collection by itself. A written dispute does, until the agency sends verification or a copy of a judgment.
  • Emailing a former resident usually depends on a transfer notice you send before placement, with an opt-out window of at least 35 days.
  • The federal text has not changed since 2021. What changed is enforcement posture and increasingly strict state and municipal overlays.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • What is a Regulation F validation notice?

    It is the written or electronic notice that carries the validation information required by 12 CFR 1006.34(c). A debt collector must provide it in its initial communication with the consumer, within five days of that initial communication, or orally in that initial communication. It identifies the collector and the consumer, names the current creditor, states the itemization date and the amount owed on it, itemizes interest, fees, payments and credits since then, gives the current balance, and tells the consumer how and by when to dispute.

  • Who sends the validation notice, the landlord or the collection agency?

    The collection agency. Regulation F binds third-party debt collectors, not landlords collecting their own rent in their own name, so the obligation attaches when you place the account. State law can still reach a landlord collecting directly. Texas is the clearest example, where Chapter 392 of the Finance Code applies to original creditors as well as third-party collectors.

  • How long does a collection agency have to send a validation notice?

    Five days after the initial communication with the consumer, read by the CFPB as five calendar days, unless the validation information was included in that initial communication or given orally during it. No notice is required if the consumer pays the debt before the notice would have been due.

  • How is the end of the thirty-day validation period calculated?

    The collector may assume the consumer receives the notice on any date at least five days after it is provided, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and federal holidays, and the validation period then ends thirty calendar days after that assumed receipt. A notice mailed Monday April 1 is assumed received Monday April 8 and the period ends Wednesday May 8. If the notice comes back undeliverable, re-sending starts a fresh calculation.

  • Can a collector keep collecting during the validation period?

    Yes, unless the consumer disputes in writing or requests the original creditor's name and address. The thirty-day window is a dispute period, not an automatic pause. What the collector must not do is engage in collection activity that overshadows or is inconsistent with the consumer's dispute rights, which is prohibited by 12 CFR 1006.38(b)(1).

  • What happens if a tenant disputes the debt in writing?

    The agency must cease collection of the debt, or the disputed portion of it, until it sends the consumer verification of the debt or a copy of a judgment. If the dispute is duplicative of an earlier written dispute, the collector may instead send a written notice saying so, with a brief statement of reasons and a reference to its earlier response.

  • Can a required field on the notice be left blank if nothing applies?

    No. Comment 1006.34(c)(2)(viii)-1 requires fields for all of the itemization items even where nothing has been assessed or applied since the itemization date. The collector may show a value of zero or none, but may not leave the field blank. For landlords this means sending ledgers with explicit zero rows rather than omitted lines.

  • What does a landlord have to supply so the notice can be produced?

    The resident's full legal name, the last known or forwarding mailing address, the property owner's legal entity name as current creditor, the lease or unit reference used as the account number, the itemization date and proof of the statement or invoice supporting it, the balance owed on that date, and an itemized ledger of interest, fees, payments, and credits since then with explicit zeroes where nothing accrued.

  • Has the validation notice rule changed recently?

    Not the federal text. Sections 1006.34, 1006.38, 1006.42 and Model Form B-1 carry 2021 amendment dates and have not been amended since. The CFPB's May 2025 withdrawal of roughly seventy guidance documents did not touch 1006.34, and the regulation, the official interpretations and the Small Entity Compliance Guide all survived. What has changed is a reduced federal enforcement posture and stricter state and municipal overlays.
